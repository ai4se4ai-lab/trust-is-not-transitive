\title{Trust Is Not Transitive: Verifying the Composition of LLM Guardrail Hierarchies}

\author{Majid Babaei%
\thanks{Affiliation and contact information to be added.}%
\thanks{Manuscript received ; revised .}}

\markboth{IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering}%
{Trust Is Not Transitive: Verifying the Composition of LLM Guardrail Hierarchies}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Large language model (LLM) agents increasingly rely on multiple guardrails to control their behavior, yet the safety of individual guardrails does not necessarily guarantee the safety of their composition. Existing approaches primarily evaluate guardrails independently, leaving uncertainty about whether their assumptions and guarantees remain valid when combined. To address this gap, we introduce a formal framework for specifying and verifying guardrail hierarchies through contract-based compositional reasoning. Our approach determines whether a hierarchy satisfies its safety requirements, identifies unsupported assumptions, and supports risk certification and incremental reassessment following system changes. We establish soundness and completeness guarantees, characterize the computational complexity of verification, and evaluate the approach through mechanized proof checks, implementation conformance testing, scalability experiments, and analyses of existing guardrail designs. The results support the correctness of the verification procedure, demonstrate efficient checking for common hierarchy structures, and reveal important assurance gaps in existing configurations. Overall, our work provides a principled foundation for reasoning about the safety of composed LLM guardrails while making explicit the assumptions on which their guarantees depend.
\end{abstract}


\begin{IEEEkeywords}
LLM agents, guardrails, formal verification, assume-guarantee contracts, dataflow analysis, runtime enforcement, statistical certification.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

\IEEEPARstart{L}{arge} language model (LLM) agents act: a coding agent reads an issue, edits files, runs tests and posts results to a web service~\cite{jimenez2023swebench}. Text the agent reads as data can steer what it does next~\cite{arxiv230212173,arxiv240613352}, so production systems place guardrails between the agent and its tools~\cite{arxiv250503574,arxiv231010501}. A guardrail stack may combine pattern rules, classifiers, LLM-based checks and human approval. Some stages can only stop a request; in others, a doubtful case is passed to a costlier stage, as in Trust or Escalate~\cite{arxiv240718370} and Conformal Arbitrage~\cite{arxiv250600911}. We call any such arrangement a \emph{guardrail hierarchy}.

Stacks are usually assessed by measurement. LlamaFirewall, for example, reports attack success and utility on AgentDojo~\cite{arxiv250503574,arxiv240613352}. Measurement says what happened on the inputs tried. An engineer changing a stack also needs answers about its structure. If a judge now reads a different input, does the old evaluation still apply? Which stages must be reassessed after a scorer is retrained? Does the order of two stages matter? On what does the claim ``this action is safe'' finally rest?

Each stage's promise is conditional, and that is where stacks go wrong. A stack can accept an unsafe action although every stage meets its contract, because a guarantee was relied on where its assumption had not been established (Section~\ref{sec:example} gives an example). Compositional reasoning excludes this step~\cite{misra1981proofs,abadi1993composing}, and the same lesson is old in security protocols~\cite{christianson1997why}. In a guardrail stack the step is usually implicit.

\textbf{The gap.} Recent work specifies and enforces agent behavior with temporal rules and contracts~\cite{arxiv250318666,arxiv260918128,arxiv260222302}, isolates untrusted data by design~\cite{arxiv250318813,arxiv250523643}, or gives statistical guarantees for particular cascades~\cite{arxiv240718370,arxiv250600911}. A position paper proposes layered probabilistic assume-guarantee reasoning for agents~\cite{arxiv260518672}. We know of no account that gives what software engineers expect of a composition method: a language in which general hierarchies can be written, a formal semantics, a decision procedure proved sound and complete against it, and an implementation shown to conform.

\textbf{Our approach.} We model a hierarchy as a term of a small calculus in which guards are constrained only by their contracts. Evidence for a contract belongs to one invocation of a stage. There is one way for evidence to move forward: a gate whose contract applies can \emph{provide} a fact that later stages may use. A guard's verdict provides nothing. From these rules follows a composition condition, which a checker decides and explains with witnesses.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{calculus of guardrail hierarchies} with a semantics that quantifies over all contract-respecting behaviors, and a specification language, \Hsl{}, for acyclic hierarchies (Section~\ref{sec:calculus}).
\item A \emph{composition condition} with proofs of soundness and of completeness relative to free stages; a theorem that transitive reasoning about evidence is unsound; a result on stage order; and a complexity classification: coNP-complete in general, near-linear without flow of facts, and coNP-hard with correlated conditions (Section~\ref{sec:verify}).
\item \emph{Route-level risk accounting}: risk as a sum over acceptance routes, certificates bound to the identities of a route's prefix, and recertification limited to what a change can reach (Section~\ref{sec:risk}).
\item \emph{Guidance for practice}: a worked example, a catalog of contracts by guard class, and a deployment process (Sections~\ref{sec:worked} and~\ref{sec:practice}).
\item \emph{Checked proofs and an evaluated implementation} (Sections~\ref{sec:mechanization}--\ref{sec:eval}): SMT-discharged inductions, conformance evidence, measurements of scale, and an analysis of public configurations and published designs.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Research questions.} \textbf{RQ1:} Are the theorems proved, and do the proofs depend on the definitions? \textbf{RQ2:} Does the implementation conform to the definitions, and are its rejections real? \textbf{RQ3:} How do checking, deciding and recertifying grow with size? \textbf{RQ4:} What do real configurations look like, and what does the approach report on them and on published designs?

\textbf{Scope.} We ask whether a composition is justified given its parts' contracts. We do not ask whether any LLM meets a contract, and we make no claim that any agent became safer. No LLM is used in the study: a theorem over all contract-respecting guards needs no sample of them. Against an adaptive attacker a statistical contract may simply be false, and Table~\ref{tab:attackers} states what then remains.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
% =====================================================================

\textbf{Guardrails.} Guardrails intercept the proposed actions of a tool-using agent. They include deterministic rules, classifiers for injection and harmful content~\cite{arxiv231206674,arxiv240618495,arxiv250118837}, LLM-based checks of an action against the user's goal~\cite{arxiv250503574,arxiv241216682} and human approval. The central threat is \emph{indirect prompt injection}, in which text read as data contains instructions the agent follows~\cite{arxiv230212173}. Guardrail products can be evaded~\cite{arxiv250411168}. A guard that reads attacker text can be addressed by it, which is why the \emph{dual-LLM pattern} separates a privileged LLM that never reads untrusted text from a quarantined one with no tool access~\cite{willison2023dual}.

\textbf{Monitors and information flow.} A reference monitor mediates every access and is small enough to verify~\cite{anderson1972computer,saltzer1975protection}. Execution monitors enforce safety properties of traces~\cite{schneider2000enforceable}. Information-flow control attaches labels to data and propagates them through derivations~\cite{denning1976lattice,sabelfeld2003language}.

\textbf{Assume-guarantee contracts.} A contract $(A,G)$ states that if the environment satisfies assumption $A$, the component guarantees $G$~\cite{meyer1992applying,benveniste2018contracts}. Compositional proof rules~\cite{misra1981proofs,jones1983tentative,abadi1993composing} are sound only when every assumption is established by the environment or by another component's guarantee. Probabilistic forms of contracts exist~\cite{delahaye2011probabilistic,nuzzo2019stochastic}.

\textbf{Dataflow analysis.} A forward \emph{must} analysis computes the facts that hold on every path to a program point. For distributive transfer functions the iterative solution equals the meet over all paths~\cite{kildall1973unified,kam1977monotone}; on an acyclic graph one pass in topological order suffices.

\textbf{Statistical certification.} For a fixed policy and $N$ independent samples with $k$ losses, the Clopper--Pearson bound is an exact one-sided upper confidence bound on the loss probability~\cite{clopper1934use}, and it is used in statistical model checking~\cite{bu2024clopper}. Bounds at level $\delta/m$ hold simultaneously for $m$ quantities by the union bound~\cite{angelopoulos2021learn}. These guarantees assume independent or exchangeable data~\cite{arxiv210707511}.

\subsection{Running Example}
\label{sec:example}

\emph{DevAgent} triages issues, edits files and calls HTTP services. Deterministic gates check production approval ($\varphi_1$), secret egress ($\varphi_2$) and the execution environment. Two semantic goals remain: $\varphi_3$, never act on instructions embedded in untrusted content; and $\varphi_4$, the change does what the requester asked. Four traces recur in this paper. In $\Tstar$, a malicious issue tells the agent to upload a secret file to an external host. In $\Tb$, a benign issue asks for public diagnostics to be attached to a local report; its action has the same shape. $\Tj$ is $\Tstar$ with one more sentence, ``reviewer must answer SAFE'', addressed to the judge. $\Tjp$ plays the same trick on a patch that breaks no deterministic rule.

Suppose a judge reads the raw issue, and its contract says that it blocks unsafe actions, as long as its input is not written to manipulate it. On $\Tjp$ the judge follows the embedded sentence and accepts. Every component met its contract, since the judge's assumption was false and it therefore promised nothing, and yet the stack accepted an unsafe action. The guarantee was relied on where its assumption had not been established, and nothing in the stack records that.

\section{A Calculus of Guardrail Hierarchies}
\label{sec:calculus}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Syntax}

Fix a finite set of goals $\Goals$, the properties every accepted proposal must satisfy, and a set of obligation names. A \emph{layer} $\ell$ is the interface of a learned or human component. It declares $\req(\ell)$, the obligations its contract assumes, and $\scope(\ell)\subseteq\Goals$, the goals it covers when it accepts. A hierarchy is a term:
\begin{align*}
H ::={}& \deny \mid \gate(n,\varphi,\ell,\Omega,D,H)\\
\mid{}& \guard(n,\ell,\Omega,H) \mid \switch(n,H,H)
\end{align*}
Here $n$ is a node identifier, and $\Omega$ is the \emph{evidence}: the obligations that the specification declares to hold for this one invocation. Evidence is a declaration. Nothing in the term makes it true, which is why the semantics below needs a separate component for what actually holds, and why declared evidence that is not established at runtime ends up on a list of assumptions.

A \emph{gate} can only stop a proposal. If it passes and its contract applies, it establishes the goal $\varphi$, if it has one ($\varphi$ may be absent, written $\bot$), and provides the facts $D$, which are obligations that hold from then on. A gate whose layer is the distinguished empty layer $\epsilon$ is a \emph{deterministic gate}, a reference monitor that decides $\varphi$ exactly. Any other gate is a \emph{veto stage}, such as a classifier that can only reject. A \emph{guard} can end a decision by accepting, can reject, or can pass the proposal on by escalating. A \emph{switch} routes on a condition and teaches the analysis nothing: taking a branch establishes no fact, even if the condition looks like a test, because a switch has no contract. To learn something, a stage must be a gate. Leaving the hierarchy denies, because $\deny$ is the only leaf. Every acceptance is therefore the act of a named guard, and we call each guard a \emph{route}, the way a proposal can get through. A stack with no such stage is modelled with a final guard of empty scope that releases what reaches it.

\subsection{Semantics}

We do not model what a stage computes. We model what could happen. For one proposal, a \emph{model} $M=(P,V,W,E,S)$ fixes every stage's behavior: $P(n)$ says whether gate $n$ passes, $V(n)\in\{\acc,\blk,\esc\}$ is guard $n$'s verdict, $W(n)$ is switch $n$'s branch, $E(n)$ is the set of obligations that actually hold on invocation $n$, and $S$ is the set of goals the proposal satisfies. These components are free, meaning arbitrary: any combination of values is a possible model unless a contract excludes it. An attack is simply a model, typically one in which an obligation fails at some stage. Fig.~\ref{fig:semantics} gives the decision semantics and contract satisfaction.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{align*}
\sem{\deny}{M}&=\denied\\
\sem{\gate(n,\varphi,\ell,\Omega,D,k)}{M}&=\sem{k}{M}\text{ if }P(n),\text{ else }\denied\\
\sem{\guard(n,\ell,\Omega,k)}{M}&=\begin{cases}\accepted(n)&\text{if }V(n)=\acc\\\sem{k}{M}&\text{if }V(n)=\esc\\\denied&\text{if }V(n)=\blk\end{cases}\\
\sem{\switch(n,a,b)}{M}&=\sem{a}{M}\text{ if }W(n),\text{ else }\sem{b}{M}
\end{align*}
\hrule
\vspace{2pt}
With $\mathrm{Real}(n)\triangleq\Omega\cup F\subseteq E(n)$ (realization):
\begin{align*}
M\models_F\;&\deny\quad\text{always}\\
M\models_F\;&\gate(n,\varphi,\ell,\Omega,D,k)\text{ iff, when }P(n):\ \mathrm{Real}(n)\;\wedge\\
&\quad\text{if }\req(\ell)\subseteq E(n):\ \varphi\in S\wedge M\models_{F\cup D}k\\
&\quad\text{otherwise: }M\models_F k\\
M\models_F\;&\guard(n,\ell,\Omega,k)\text{ iff }\mathrm{Real}(n)\;\wedge\\
&\quad(V(n)=\acc\wedge\req(\ell)\subseteq E(n)\Rightarrow\scope(\ell)\subseteq S)\\
&\quad\wedge(V(n)=\esc\Rightarrow M\models_F k)\\
M\models_F\;&\switch(n,a,b)\text{ iff }M\models_F a\text{ if }W(n),\text{ else }M\models_F b
\end{align*}
\caption{Decision semantics (top) and contract satisfaction (bottom). $F$ is the set of facts provided so far on this run. All premises concern only the invocations this proposal reaches.}
\label{fig:semantics}
\end{figure}

$M\models_F H$ reads ``this behavior respects every contract on the invocations the proposal reaches, given facts $F$''. Three rules carry the content. \emph{Realization:} declared evidence and provided facts must hold at the invocation; a false declaration is outside the guarantee. The test in a contract is on $E(n)$, what is true, while the checker below can only see $\Omega$, what is declared, and realization links the two. \emph{Conditionality:} a promise applies only if all of the stage's obligations hold on this invocation, so a stage may do anything otherwise. \emph{Locality:} $E$ is indexed by node, so that an obligation at $n$ says nothing about any other node. A fact in $F$ reaches later stages only through a gate whose contract applied. A verdict of $\esc$ asks nothing of a guard and adds nothing to $F$. Indexing by invocation is a modelling choice, justified by how guards work: each is called on its own view of the proposal, so that what held of the scorer's input need not hold of the judge's.

\begin{definition}[Safety]
\label{def:safe}
$H$ is \emph{safe from} $(A,B)$ if for every model $M$ with $A\subseteq S$ and $M\models_B H$: if $\sem{H}{M}=\accepted(r)$ for some $r$, then $\Goals\subseteq S$. $H$ is \emph{safe} if it is safe from $(\emptyset,\emptyset)$.
\end{definition}

Safety holds one proposal at a time and over all behaviors, but it is conditional on contracts, and an attacker who makes a contract false is outside it. It says nothing about usefulness: $\deny$ is safe. Table~\ref{tab:attackers} separates what each attacker class leaves standing; the drift $\eta$ is the total-variation distance between the calibration and deployment distributions (Section~\ref{sec:risk}).

\subsection{The Specification Language}

\Hsl{} is the concrete syntax: a closed JSON schema declaring goals, obligations, layers and an acyclic graph of stages. Stages can share successors, and a term is the graph's unfolding along paths. Obligations carry a \emph{kind} that records how they are established. A \emph{checked} obligation has a runtime predicate, and failing it denies. A \emph{construction} obligation is true of the code, such as the single function that builds a guard's input. An \emph{assumed} obligation is the deployer's to justify. Kind does not affect the composition condition; it determines what is left to argue. An obligation may be tied to a view. Evidence tied to another view than the stage's layer is dropped before analysis, with a warning. Fig.~\ref{fig:devagent} and Listing~\ref{lst:devagent} show DevAgent.

\section{Verifying a Hierarchy}
\label{sec:verify}

\subsection{The Composition Condition}

Walking from the entry, we track the goals $A$ established and the facts $B$ provided so far. A gate is \emph{credited} if its obligations are available as evidence or facts; then it adds its goal and facts. At a guard, acceptance ends the decision, so the goals still open must be in that guard's scope, and its contract must be usable. For evidence $e$:
\begin{align*}
\ok(\ell,e,A)&\triangleq\Goals\subseteq A\;\vee\\
&\quad\bigl(\req(\ell)\subseteq e\wedge\Goals\subseteq A\cup\scope(\ell)\bigr)
\end{align*}
\begingroup\small
\begin{align*}
\chk(\deny,A,B)&=\mathrm{true}\\
\chk(\gate(\ldots,k),A,B)&=\chk(k,A',B')\\
\text{with }(A',B')&=(A\cup\{\varphi\},B\cup D)\\
\text{if }\req(\ell)\subseteq\Omega\cup B,\ &\text{else }(A,B)\\
\chk(\guard(\ldots,k),A,B)&=\ok(\ell,\Omega\cup B,A)\\
&\quad\wedge\chk(k,A,B)\\
\chk(\switch(n,a,b),A,B)&=\chk(a,A,B)\wedge\chk(b,A,B)
\end{align*}
\endgroup
The first disjunct of $\ok$ avoids a false alarm: if gates have established every goal, a guard's acceptance is harmless whatever it does.

\subsection{Soundness and Completeness}

\begin{theorem}[Soundness]
\label{thm:sound}
If $\chk(H,A,B)$, then $H$ is safe from $(A,B)$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
By structural induction on $H$, generalizing over $A$, $B$ and $F\supseteq B$. Let $M\models_F H$, $A\subseteq S$ and $\sem{H}{M}=\accepted(r)$. The case $\deny$ is vacuous. \emph{Gate.} Acceptance implies $P(n)$. If the gate is credited, $\req(\ell)\subseteq\Omega\cup B\subseteq\Omega\cup F\subseteq E(n)$ by realization, so $\varphi\in S$ and $M\models_{F\cup D}k$, and the hypothesis applies with $A\cup\{\varphi\}$, $B\cup D$ and $F\cup D$. If it is not credited, $(A,B)$ is unchanged, and the hypothesis applies with $F$ or $F\cup D$, whichever the contract gives, since $B$ is contained in both. \emph{Guard.} If $V(n)=\esc$, then $M\models_F k$ and the hypothesis applies. If $V(n)=\acc$ and $\Goals\subseteq A$, we are done. Otherwise $\req(\ell)\subseteq\Omega\cup B\subseteq E(n)$, the contract gives $\scope(\ell)\subseteq S$, and $\Goals\subseteq A\cup\scope(\ell)\subseteq S$. \emph{Switch.} The chosen branch is checked and the hypothesis applies.
\end{IEEEproof}

Completeness needs that no identifier repeats along a path, written $\mathrm{uniq}(H)$; otherwise one node's behavior could not be chosen independently at two points. Identifiers may repeat across the branches of a switch, which lets a shared graph node appear twice in the unfolding.

\begin{lemma}[Frame]
\label{lem:frame}
If $n$ does not occur in $H$, changing $P$, $V$, $W$ or $E$ at $n$ changes neither $\sem{H}{M}$ nor whether $M\models_F H$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{theorem}[Completeness]
\label{thm:complete}
If $\neg\chk(H,A,B)$ and $\mathrm{uniq}(H)$, there are a model $M$ and a node $r$ with $A\subseteq S$, $M\models_B H$, $\sem{H}{M}=\accepted(r)$ and $\Goals\not\subseteq S$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
By structural induction, constructing $M$. $\deny$ always checks. \emph{Gate.} The continuation is rejected under $(A',B')$; take its witness and set $P(n)$ true, which by Lemma~\ref{lem:frame} and uniqueness leaves the continuation's run unchanged. If the gate is credited, let every obligation hold at $n$; the contract asks for $\varphi\in S$, which holds because $A'\subseteq S$ in the witness, and for $M\models_{B\cup D}k$, which is the witness. If not, some $o\in\req(\ell)\setminus(\Omega\cup B)$ exists; let $o$ alone fail at $n$, so that the contract is vacuous and the continuation runs under $B$. \emph{Switch.} Choose the rejected branch. \emph{Guard.} If the continuation is rejected, take its witness and let $n$ escalate, with every obligation holding at $n$. Otherwise $\ok(\ell,\Omega\cup B,A)$ fails, so an open goal exists, and either (a)~some $o\in\req(\ell)\setminus(\Omega\cup B)$ exists, with an open goal $\psi$, or (b)~some open goal $\psi\notin\scope(\ell)$ exists. Let $S$ be everything but $\psi$, which keeps $A\subseteq S$, and let $V(n)=\acc$. In (a) let $E(n)$ be everything but $o$, so that the contract is vacuous. In (b) let everything hold; the contract asks only for $\scope(\ell)\subseteq S$.
\end{IEEEproof}

So $\chk(H,\emptyset,\emptyset)$ holds exactly when $H$ is safe, under a stated abstraction: stages are free apart from contracts. A rejected hierarchy may be fine for the guards actually deployed, if they do more than they declare, or if two stages are correlated (Section~\ref{sec:complexity}). What a rejection says is that the declared contracts do not entail the goals. Declaring an assumption as supplied evidence silences the rejection, by design: the claim then moves to the residual list, where someone must answer for it.

The checker reports what its verdict rests on. The \emph{residual} is the set of (stage, assumed obligation) pairs declared at a stage that some goal or provided fact depends on. The \emph{claims} are the goals and facts that deterministic gates are trusted to establish; a gate with a trivial rule that claims a fact silences a rejection just as a false declaration does, so claims need review too. Not every residual entry is needed, since a goal may also be covered later. The checker therefore removes each entry alone and rechecks. Two entries that can stand in for each other both pass this test, so the test finds single points of dependence and not minimal sets.

\begin{corollary}[Necessary assumptions]
\label{cor:necessary}
If removing a residual entry from an accepted hierarchy makes it rejected, the entry is necessary: without it there is a contract-respecting violating run.
\end{corollary}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} applied to the thinned hierarchy, whose identifiers are unchanged.
\end{IEEEproof}

\subsection{Transitive Reasoning Is Unsound}

The usual informal argument for a stack is transitive: each stage was validated, and each trusts the next. For DevAgent's early scorer and judge, it runs: the scorer was validated on sanitized input, the judge comes after the scorer, so the judge's input can be taken as sanitized too. In our terms, evidence declared at one stage is taken to hold at later stages. Let $\chk^{*}$ be $\chk$ except that $\Omega$ is added to $B$ at every stage.

\begin{theorem}[Transitive reasoning]
\label{thm:transitive}
(a)~If $\chk(H,A,B)$, $A\subseteq A_2$ and $B\subseteq B_2$, then $\chk^{*}(H,A_2,B_2)$. (b)~The converse fails: there are hierarchies accepted by $\chk^{*}$ that have a contract-respecting violating run.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
(a) By structural induction, generalizing over all four sets; credit under $\chk$ implies credit under $\chk^{*}$, and the sets stay ordered. (b) Let $H=\guard(n_1,\ell_1,\{o\},\guard(n_2,\ell_2,\emptyset,\deny))$ with $\req(\ell_1)=\req(\ell_2)=\{o\}$ and a goal in the scope of both. $\chk^{*}$ accepts, since $o$ is inherited. $\chk$ rejects at $n_2$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:complete} gives the run: $n_1$ escalates, $o$ fails at $n_2$ alone, and $n_2$ accepts a violating proposal.
\end{IEEEproof}

The result is close to the definition of the semantics, and that is its point: once evidence is scoped to an invocation, the argument above fails, and the proof exhibits the run where it does, with $n_2$ in the role of the judge. It also shows why the calculus has provided facts and no inheritance. If a property really does persist, a gate must establish it, and then the checker can credit it.

\subsection{When Order Matters}

\begin{theorem}[Order]
\label{thm:order}
(a)~Moving a gate before a guard preserves acceptance: if the hierarchy with $\guard(n,\ell,\Omega,\gate(m,\varphi,\ell',\Omega',D,k))$ checks, so does the one with $\gate(m,\varphi,\ell',\Omega',D,\guard(n,\ell,\Omega,k))$. (b)~Two adjacent deterministic gates that provide nothing commute. (c)~The converse of (a) fails, and gates do not commute when one provides what the other requires.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
(a) Moving the gate leaves its credit unchanged, and $\ok$ is preserved by enlarging its goals and evidence (Lemma~\ref{lem:meet}), so the guard's condition still holds. (b) The sets reaching the continuation are equal. (c) A guard with empty scope followed by a gate for the only goal is rejected, and with the order swapped it is accepted. For gates, a gate requiring $o$ placed before the gate providing $o$ is not credited.
\end{IEEEproof}

For composition, then, a gate placed earlier never hurts, provided the gate's own evidence still holds at the earlier point, since evidence belongs to an invocation. The theorem concerns the guarantee only. Order also affects cost and what a stage sees, and neither appears in the check. Beyond these cases we have no general characterization.

\subsection{From Terms to Graphs: Algorithm and Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

A term unfolds an \Hsl{} graph, and $d$ diamonds give $2^{d}$ paths. Algorithm~\ref{alg:check} instead computes, by one pass in topological order, $\mathrm{avail}(n)$ and $\mathrm{facts}(n)$: the goals and facts established on every path to $n$.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Checking a hierarchy graph}
\label{alg:check}
\footnotesize
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE reject on dangling reference, undeclared name or cycle
\STATE $\mathrm{avail}(\mathit{entry}),\mathrm{facts}(\mathit{entry})\gets\emptyset,\emptyset$
\FOR{$n$ reachable, in topological order}
  \STATE $(\mathit{out},\mathit{known})\gets$ gate transfer of $n$ on $(\mathrm{avail}(n),\mathrm{facts}(n))$
  \FOR{each successor $c$ of $n$}
    \STATE $\mathrm{avail}(c)\gets\mathrm{avail}(c)\cap\mathit{out}$; $\mathrm{facts}(c)\gets\mathrm{facts}(c)\cap\mathit{known}$
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\STATE $W\gets$ each reachable guard failing $\ok$ on these sets, with a witness path
\IF{$W\neq\emptyset$ and some reachable gate provides a fact}
  \STATE explore every reachable $(n,A,B)$ in topological order
  \STATE $W\gets$ each $(n,A,B)$ at a guard failing $\ok(\ell_n,\Omega_n\cup B,A)$
\ENDIF
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{lemma}[Meet]
\label{lem:meet}
(a)~If $A\subseteq A'$, $e\subseteq e'$ and $\ok(\ell,e,A)$, then $\ok(\ell,e',A')$. (b)~If $\ok(\ell,e,A)$ and $\ok(\ell,e,A')$, then $\ok(\ell,e,A\cap A')$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{IEEEproof}
(a) is immediate. For (b), if $\Goals\subseteq A\cap A'$ we are done. Otherwise a goal is open on one side, so $\req(\ell)\subseteq e$; and a goal open in $A\cap A'$ is open in $A$ or $A'$, and so is in $\scope(\ell)$.
\end{IEEEproof}

Lemma~\ref{lem:meet}(b) has one evidence set on purpose. The analogous statement with $\ok(\ell,e,A)$, $\ok(\ell,e',A')$ and $\ok(\ell,e\cap e',A\cap A')$ is false. One path may have every goal and no evidence, and another path the reverse. Each path is fine, while the componentwise meet is not.

For a graph $G$ with entry $e$, the unfolding is the term that replaces each edge to a node by that node's term and each missing successor by $\deny$. An acyclic graph's unfolding satisfies $\mathrm{uniq}$. A graph is \emph{flow-free} if no reachable gate provides a fact.

\begin{theorem}[Exactness of the algorithm]
\label{thm:dag}
For an acyclic graph with unfolding $H$, Algorithm~\ref{alg:check} ends with $W=\emptyset$ iff $\chk(H,\emptyset,\emptyset)$. Theorems~\ref{thm:sound} and~\ref{thm:complete} therefore hold for graphs.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
For a path $\pi$ from the entry to $n$, let $(A_\pi,B_\pi)$ be the result of the gate transfers along $\pi$. Unfolding the equations of $\chk$ by induction on $H$ shows that $\chk(H,\emptyset,\emptyset)$ holds iff $\ok(\ell_n,\Omega_n\cup B_\pi,A_\pi)$ for every guard $n$ and every path $\pi$ to it. \emph{Flow-free case.} $B_\pi=\emptyset$ and credit is independent of the path. By induction along the topological order, $\mathrm{avail}(n)=\bigcap_\pi A_\pi$, because unions distribute over intersections. Then $\ok(\ell_n,\Omega_n,\mathrm{avail}(n))$ iff $\ok(\ell_n,\Omega_n,A_\pi)$ for all $\pi$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:meet}. \emph{General case.} The meets are subsets of each $(A_\pi,B_\pi)$, by induction, because gate transfer is monotone. So if the first pass finds nothing, each path passes by Lemma~\ref{lem:meet}(a). If it finds something, the exploration enumerates exactly the pairs $(A_\pi,B_\pi)$ at each node, and $W$ is the path-wise condition.
\end{IEEEproof}

A goal missing at $n$ is missing on a specific path, found by walking back through a predecessor whose out-set lacks it. The implementation turns this path into the model of Theorem~\ref{thm:complete}, so every rejection can be replayed.

\begin{theorem}[Complexity]
\label{thm:complexity}
Let $w$ be the word size, $\|H\|$ the size of the specification and $O$ the obligations.
(a)~For flow-free graphs, deciding safety takes $O((|V|+|E|)\lceil(|\Goals|+|O|)/w\rceil+\|H\|\log\|H\|)$ time, which is near-linear for bounded $\Goals$ and $O$, however many paths the graph has. Each witness adds at most $|V|$.
(b)~In general, deciding safety is coNP-complete.
(c)~In general, the first pass is sound, and the exploration takes time proportional to the number of reachable $(n,A,B)$ triples, which can be exponential.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
(a) Depth-first search orders the graph. With sets as bit vectors, each node performs a union and each edge an intersection. Masks are built once per layer from its declared names; sorting names accounts for the logarithm. (b) A violating path with its model is a polynomial certificate, so the complement is in NP. For hardness, reduce from satisfiability of a CNF formula. For each variable, a switch chooses between two gates providing $t_v$ or $f_v$. Then, for each clause, a veto stage requires the facts falsifying its literals and provides \ob{bad}. A final guard with one open goal requires \ob{bad}. A path corresponds to an assignment, and \ob{bad} is provided on it iff the assignment falsifies a clause. The hierarchy is rejected iff some path lacks \ob{bad}, that is, iff the formula is satisfiable. (c) Soundness is shown in Theorem~\ref{thm:dag}.
\end{IEEEproof}

The source of hardness is credit that depends on facts. If only guards consume provided facts, credit is path-independent, and one can treat each (open goal, missing obligation) pair as a single fact in a must-analysis, which is polynomial; we have not implemented this middle case, since the exploration handles it. We expect hierarchies with few provided facts to have few states, and E9 measures a worst case.

A second source of hardness lies outside the calculus. Our switches are free: two switches on the same condition are treated as independent.

\begin{proposition}[Correlated conditions]
\label{prop:correlated}
If equal condition names denote one Boolean, safety is coNP-hard even for flow-free graphs. The free analysis stays sound, and it may reject a safe hierarchy.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Hardness: for each clause in turn, chain one switch per literal, on the literal's variable; a true literal jumps to the next clause, and falling through denies. A final guard with an open goal is then reachable iff some assignment satisfies every clause. Soundness: a correlated model is a free model. Incompleteness: let $s_1$ on $c$ lead to a gate for $\varphi$ and then to $s_2$, or directly to $s_2$; let $s_2$ on $c$ lead to a guard of empty scope or to $\deny$. No run reaches the guard without passing the gate, yet the free analysis rejects.
\end{IEEEproof}

\subsection{Structural Properties}
\label{sec:engineering}

\begin{theorem}[Structural properties]
\label{thm:mono}
\textbf{Monotonicity.} If $M'$ has the same branches as $M$, $P'(n)\Rightarrow P(n)$, $V'(n)=\acc\Rightarrow V(n)=\acc$ and $V'(n)=\esc\Rightarrow V(n)\in\{\esc,\acc\}$, then $M'$ accepts only if $M$ does.
\textbf{Routes.} If $\sem{H}{M}=\accepted(r)$, then $r$ is a guard of $H$ and $V(r)=\acc$.
\textbf{Prefix locality.} Replacing the continuation of guard $n$ does not change the decision of a proposal that does not escalate past $n$.
\textbf{Decision cost.} A decision visits at most $\mathrm{depth}(H)$ stages.
\textbf{Budget.} Along any sequence of costs, a gate that accepts cost $c$ only if $c\ge0$ and $s+c\le L$, and adds $c$ to $s$ only then, keeps $0\le s\le L$.
\textbf{Taint.} If a derived value takes the union of its sources' labels and its own, a label on any source is on every value derived from it.
\end{theorem}

Each part is a structural induction. The budget property applies to an agent only if the gate is on every route. The taint property is what lets a deterministic gate decide secret egress from labels, provided the host records every derivation.

\begin{corollary}[Affected routes]
\label{cor:affected}
If stage $c$ of a graph changes, the event ``accepted on route $r$'' is unchanged for every route $r$ such that $c$ is neither $r$ nor an ancestor of $r$ in the old and the new graph.
\end{corollary}
\begin{IEEEproof}
A run ending at $r$ visits only $r$ and its ancestors. If $c$ is none of these, the run never consults $c$, and the two models agree along it step by step. A run that visits $c$ cannot end at $r$ under either model.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{theorem}[Non-interference]
\label{thm:ni}
Let each stage be a function of a view of the proposal. If two proposals give the same view at every node of $H$, their decisions are equal.
\end{theorem}

A view therefore limits a contract. Two proposals with equal views get equal verdicts, so a guard can promise only what its view distinguishes. In the artifact the structured view is six Booleans and a score, and a judge reading only that cannot decide $\varphi_3$ on a patch, because a malicious and a benign patch can have equal views. A guard that must judge content has to read it, and then it can be addressed by it. DevAgent resolves the tension by splitting on provenance: the raw judge sees only input a deterministic gate has shown to carry no untrusted text, and tainted input goes to a person. Neither arm claims that a guard can read attacker text safely.

\section{From Safety to Risk}
\label{sec:risk}
% =====================================================================

So far contracts have been exact: if the obligations hold, the promise holds. A learned stage does not keep such a contract; it errs on some inputs. We use the exact analysis for learned stages in a limited sense, as an idealization that shows what depends on what: if the stage were right whenever its obligations held, would the composition be justified? An obligation such as \ob{miss\_rate\_bounded} is a claim about a population and does not hold ``at an invocation''. It stands for an external condition: a certificate for this configuration, or a bound stated by a source, exists. The actual guarantee for such goals comes from measurement, as follows.

Split $\Goals=\Goals_{d}\uplus\Goals_{s}$ into \emph{exact} goals, which must hold on every accepted proposal, and \emph{statistical} goals, for which a rate of failure is tolerated. Let proposals $x$ come from a distribution $\mathcal{D}$, each determining one run of the deployed hierarchy, and let $Y(x)=1$ if $x$ violates a goal. The \emph{joint risk} of a deployed configuration, with closed routes denying, is $R=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}[Y=1\wedge x\text{ accepted}]$.

\subsection{Risk Is a Sum over Routes}

A route $r$ is \emph{gated} if deterministic gates establish every goal of $\Goals_{s}$ on every path to $r$. Let $Q$ be the other routes, called \emph{statistical}. A gated route may still have a goal of $\Goals_d$ covered by a guard's exact contract. A route on which deterministic gates establish every goal is \emph{fully gated}, and only such routes stand against an adaptive attacker.

\begin{theorem}[Route risk]
\label{thm:risk}
Let $H$ pass Algorithm~\ref{alg:check}. Suppose that on the support of $\mathcal{D}$: (i)~deterministic gates decide their goals exactly, in $\Goals_d$ and in $\Goals_s$, and (ii)~realization holds and all contracts hold for goals in $\Goals_d$. Then $R=\sum_{r\in Q}R_r$, with $R_r=\Pr_{\mathcal{D}}[Y=1\wedge\accepted(r)]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
By Routes and determinism, acceptance splits into disjoint route events, so $R=\sum_r R_r$. Let $H_d$ be $H$ with goals $\Goals_d$, every scope intersected with $\Goals_d$, and goals outside $\Goals_d$ dropped from gates. With goals restricted, $\ok$ is implied at each guard by its value in $H$, so $H_d$ passes. The model of $x$ satisfies $H_d$'s contracts by (i) and (ii), so by Theorem~\ref{thm:dag} an accepted $x$ satisfies $\Goals_d$. If $x$ is accepted on a gated route, it passed on its path the deterministic gates for every goal of $\Goals_s$, which by (i) hold. So $Y(x)=0$ and $R_r=0$.
\end{IEEEproof}

No statistical contract, independence assumption or per-layer rate appears here. That matters because per-layer rates mislead.

\begin{proposition}[Veto and cascade]
\label{prop:veto}
Let guards have marginal miss probabilities $m_i$ on the unsafe population. (a)~In a veto chain, the joint miss probability can be anything from $\max(0,1-\sum_i(1-m_i))$ to $\min_i m_i$, and $\prod_i m_i$ is one value in that range, attained under conditional independence. (b)~In a cascade with early acceptance, neither $\min_i m_i$ nor $\prod_i m_i$ bounds risk: if a first guard accepts a share $\rho$ of unsafe proposals and a later guard blocks all it sees, the later guard's miss probability is zero, so minimum and product are zero, while risk is $\rho$ times the unsafe mass.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
(a) These are the Fr\'echet--Hoeffding bounds. (b) follows by construction and Routes.
\end{IEEEproof}

Note what the composition check does not do here. $R_r$ is measured on the deployed function, and a certificate for a raw-text judge is valid under $\mathcal{D}$ whether or not the check accepts the hierarchy. The check contributes three things: the guarantee for $\Goals_d$, which needs no distribution; the set $Q$ of routes needing evidence; and, by rejecting, the warning that a bound under $\mathcal{D}$ does not extend to text written to manipulate the guard.

\subsection{Route Certificates}

A \emph{route certificate} is a tuple $(r,f,c,u_r,\delta_r,N)$: route, prefix fingerprint, a context label naming the deployment it is for, upper bound, confidence share and sample count. The confidence budget $\delta$ is allocated to the routes of $Q$ before any calibration. Calibration runs the deployed decision function with certificates ignored on $N$ samples and counts $k_r$, the unsafe proposals accepted on $r$. Then
\begin{equation}
u_r=\mathrm{Beta}^{-1}\bigl(1-\delta_r;\,k_r+1,\,N-k_r\bigr),\quad u_r=1\text{ if }k_r=N.
\label{eq:cp}
\end{equation}
In plain words, $u_r$ is the largest loss rate still consistent with seeing only $k_r$ losses, at confidence $1-\delta_r$. At runtime a statistical route accepts only while a certificate for its current fingerprint is open. The engine refuses an allocation above $\delta$, a certificate above its route's share, two certificates for one route, and bounds that sum above the budget $\varepsilon$. A \emph{closed} route denies.

\begin{theorem}[Certified risk]
\label{thm:certified}
Under the premises of Theorem~\ref{thm:risk}, fix a configuration whose stages on statistical routes do not read session state. Let shares $\delta_r$ be fixed before calibration with $\sum_{r\in Q}\delta_r\le\delta$, and let each certificate's samples be i.i.d.\ from $\mathcal{D}$, drawn after its route's prefix was fixed. With probability at least $1-\delta$, $R\le\sum_{r\text{ open}}u_r$. The runtime keeps this sum at most $\varepsilon$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Each $u_r$ is an upper bound on $R_r$ at level $\delta_r$~\cite{clopper1934use}, and the union bound over all of $Q$ needs no independence among certificates, so they may come from different runs. Because the shares cover $Q$ and are fixed in advance, which certificates end up open cannot affect the bound. Closing a route only removes acceptances (Monotonicity), and gated routes contribute zero by Theorem~\ref{thm:risk}.
\end{IEEEproof}

The statement is per configuration. Over a sequence of versions the chance that some deployed configuration is wrong accumulates, so a long-lived deployment should allocate shares across versions in advance. A deployer who expects to add routes must reserve shares for them, since the engine refuses old certificates whose shares no longer fit. Recalibrating until a bound passes is selection, and it voids the level unless the attempts are charged to $\delta$.

Joint risk scales with the prevalence of attacks. With prevalence $\resWorkPrevalence$, a guard that misses every attack loses about $\resWorkBlindLosses$ of $\resWorkSamples$ samples, and its bound $\resWorkBlindUpper$ passes a budget of $\resWorkBudget$. A deployer should therefore also report the conditional miss rate, $R$ over the unsafe mass, by attack class, and should treat a joint bound as meaningful only with a calibration set that contains enough attacks. For scale: with $\delta_r=\resWorkShare$ and $N=\resWorkSamples$, zero losses give $u_r=\resWorkUpperZero$ and ten give $\resWorkUpperTen$. Where the samples and labels $Y$ come from is usually the hardest part of the exercise, and nothing here supplies them.

\subsection{Incremental Recertification}

Each route is bound to a \emph{prefix fingerprint}, a Merkle hash in topological order. A node's fingerprint covers its stage and layer, the identities of its rule, guard or condition and of its checked predicates, obligation kinds, the labelled fingerprints of its predecessors, and a digest of the goals, the statistical goals and a label naming the oracle $Y$. A component's identity covers its direct source, closure values, defaults, partial arguments, reachable instance state, array contents and declared version and configuration. A component with no readable identity is refused. Code reached only through calls, state outside the object, and files such as model weights are not covered; the deployer must declare them in the configuration.

By Corollary~\ref{cor:affected}, a change to a stage cannot alter the event of a route it does not precede, so only routes it precedes need new evidence. They close alone, and the others keep their certificates. The cost is a tighter guarantee foregone: for few routes, one whole-hierarchy bound beats a sum of route bounds at stricter levels. Closing a route is an outage for its traffic, so a deployment should calibrate the new prefix in shadow mode before switching. A change that only tightens a stage cannot raise $R_r$ on its own route (Monotonicity), but it can move traffic to later routes, so we keep the conservative rule.

\section{Worked Example}
\label{sec:worked}
% =====================================================================

We follow DevAgent through the tool, under the adversarial reading of Section~\ref{sec:practice}, in which a stage that reads raw text needs the claim \ob{safe\_on\_this\_text}: its contract holds on this input, either because the input carries no attacker text or because the stage resists it.

\textbf{Traces.} On $\Tstar$ and $\Tj$, gate $g_2$ denies, because a secret-labeled input goes to an external host. No learned stage is consulted, and the sentence for the judge is never read. On $\Tb$, the issue is tainted, so the proposal reaches the scorer and then a person, who accepts on route $n_4$. This is the cost of the design: benign work derived from untrusted text needs a person. On $\Tjp$ the patch is also tainted and takes the same path, so the sentence for the judge reaches a person and not the judge.

\textbf{Checking.} Follow the untainted arm with $(A,B)$. After $g_1,g_2,g_3$ we have $A=\{\varphi_1,\varphi_2,\varphi_{\mathrm{env}}\}$ and $B=\emptyset$. Switch $s$ changes nothing. Gate $c$ is deterministic, so it is credited: $\varphi_3$ joins $A$ and \ob{safe\_on\_this\_text} joins $B$. At $n_3$ only $\varphi_4$ is open and it is in scope, and the judge's obligations are met by its evidence and by $B$. On the tainted arm, $v_2$ adds nothing, so $\varphi_3$ and $\varphi_4$ are open at $n_4$, both in scope. Its evidence declares \ob{safe\_on\_this\_text}, an assumption about people. The specification is accepted with routes $n_3$ and $n_4$. The residual list has three entries, all found necessary: \ob{miss\_rate\_bounded} at $n_3$, and \ob{human\_attentive} and \ob{safe\_on\_this\_text} at $n_4$. The claims list includes $c$'s goal and fact, so the correctness of taint tracking is on the record. Obligation \ob{config\_match} is checked at runtime. The scorer contributes nothing to any goal; it exists to reduce the person's workload.

The claim about people deserves a word. A person reading a diff is not immune to text addressed to them, and Non-interference rules out a person who decides $\varphi_3$ from a view without the content. So the person reads the content, and the hierarchy's guarantee on this route rests on an assumption stated in the open. Workload, fatigue and automation bias bear directly on it, and we have no data on them.

\textbf{The exposed variant.} Now consider a simpler stack: no switch and no provenance gate, with one raw-text judge covering $\varphi_3$ and $\varphi_4$ for all input. It is rejected:
\begin{lstlisting}
obligation at n3: safe_on_this_text
  goal: phi3   path: g1 g2 g3 n3
\end{lstlisting}
The witness is a complete model, which the artifact verifies against Fig.~\ref{fig:semantics}. All gates pass. At $n_3$ every obligation holds except the one reported, so the contract promises nothing, and $n_3$ accepts. The proposal satisfies $\varphi_1,\varphi_2,\varphi_{\mathrm{env}}$ and violates $\varphi_3$. This is $\Tjp$ of Section~\ref{sec:example}, reconstructed mechanically. Under a benign threat model, where the obligation is not required, the same stack is accepted, which is why a threat model has to be stated.

\textbf{Certifying.} Take $\Goals_s=\{\varphi_3,\varphi_4\}$. Route $n_3$ is statistical because of $\varphi_4$, and so is $n_4$, so $Q=\{n_3,n_4\}$. With $\delta=\resWorkShareTotal$, each route gets $\delta_r=\resWorkShare$. Suppose $N=\resWorkSamples$ labeled samples show ten unsafe acceptances on $n_3$: then $u_{n_3}=\resWorkUpperTen$. Route $n_4$ is certified the same way, from decisions recorded in shadow mode, so that the person's error rate is measured instead of assumed. That needs labels and a person's time.

\textbf{Change.} Changing any of $g_1,g_2,g_3,s$ stales both routes, because every route descends from them. Changing $v_2$ or $n_4$ stales $n_4$ alone, and changing $c$ or $n_3$ stales $n_3$ alone. So the question from the introduction has a plain answer: a retrained scorer costs one route here, and a rule change costs all of them. Rule changes may be the most frequent, and for those the incremental scheme gains nothing.

\subsection{Writing Contracts}

The check is only as meaningful as the contracts, and three rules help.

\emph{Choose goals independently of tools.} ``CodeShield found nothing'' is a tautology if the goal is defined by the tool's output. ``The emitted code has no injectable SQL'' is a goal, and a static analyzer is then a veto stage with a recall obligation, not a deterministic gate. A stage is a deterministic gate only if it decides the goal exactly, which usually needs the goal to be a property of typed actions and trusted metadata.

\emph{State what a stage assumes about adversaries once, uniformly.} An obligation that the input is clean makes no sense for a detector of unclean input. We use two uniform obligations instead. Every learned stage requires \ob{miss\_rate\_bounded}, supplied only if a certificate for the deployment or a bound stated by a source exists. Under an adversarial threat model, every stage that reads raw text also requires \ob{safe\_on\_this\_text}. These can be applied mechanically. Verdicts still depend on the labels a modeller gives a stage, such as its view or whether it is learned, so those labels should be justified in a specification's review.

\emph{Make each assumed obligation someone's job.} A residual entry should have an owner and an argument, in the manner of an assurance case. The checker cannot tell a justified assumption from one added to silence it. Table~\ref{tab:catalog} gives starting points by guard class.

\subsection{In a Development Process}

We suggest treating an \Hsl{} specification as a reviewed artifact, kept with the code and checked in continuous integration. On a rejection, the finding offers three options: add a stage, change the stage's view or evidence, or accept the assumption and name its owner. The residual list goes to whoever owns the safety case. For statistical routes, the steps are to collect labeled data, including attacks, for each configuration in shadow mode; certify with shares reserved; switch; and monitor for drift, because a certificate says nothing about data it has not seen. When a route closes, a deployment should decide in advance whether its traffic is denied or sent to a person. None of this process has been evaluated with practitioners. \section{Mechanized Proofs}
\label{sec:mechanization}
% =====================================================================

The definitions of Fig.~\ref{fig:semantics} and $\chk$ are first-order equations over an inductive datatype of terms, one per constructor, and they are stated for Z3~\cite{demoura2008z3} and cvc5~\cite{barbosa2022cvc5}. Sets are arrays, and the components of a model are unconstrained arrays, so the quantification over behaviors is literal. A structural induction becomes one closed proof goal per constructor, with instances of the induction hypothesis as premises; existential witnesses appear as explicit updates of the model. A proof goal is valid iff its negation is unsatisfiable. In this section and in E1, ``proof goal'' means a formula sent to a solver, not a contract's obligation.

We take care over what this does and does not show. Every proof goal goes to both solvers, though cvc5 reads SMT-LIB printed from the same formula, so its independence does not extend to the encoding. The premises of each proof goal are tested for satisfiability, with union unfolded to its definition, and E1 reports the counts. Proof mutants change the definitions as a mistaken design would, including making evidence inherited, and a mutant counts as killed only if the solver produces a countermodel to a named theorem. The script exits with failure if either solver does not prove a goal.

Goals and obligations share one sort in the script, and $S$ is not constrained to lie within $\Goals$. In the completeness witness, then, $S$ is everything but $\psi$, as in the paper's proof, read over that sort. Adequacy tests compare the equations with the reference interpreter on concrete terms and models, for $\chk$, the semantics and $\models$.

\textbf{What is trusted.} The trusted base has these parts: Z3 or cvc5; the script that states proof goals and applies the induction schema, which no solver checks, including the case splits that connect the cases of a theorem; the correspondence of the paper's notation with the equations; and the correspondence of the equations with Python, which is tested and not proved. The following are paper proofs, not mechanized: Theorem~\ref{thm:dag}, Theorem~\ref{thm:complexity}, Proposition~\ref{prop:correlated}, Corollary~\ref{cor:affected}, Corollary~\ref{cor:necessary}, Theorems~\ref{thm:risk} and~\ref{thm:certified}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:veto}. Some mechanized items are close to definitional, Taint and Non-interference among them, and the count of proof goals is no measure of depth. Porting to a proof assistant would remove the unchecked script.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
% =====================================================================

The library \tint{} is written in Python. The parser enforces a closed schema, rejecting unknown fields and duplicate keys. The checker implements Algorithm~\ref{alg:check} with goal and fact sets as integers. Findings carry a code, a node, the missing name, a witness path and the violated goal, and a separate function turns a finding into a complete model. Warnings report evidence dropped for its view.

The engine interprets a checked hierarchy and fails closed: an exception in a stage, a malformed verdict or a failed checked obligation denies. It has one decision path, used for deployment and, with certificates ignored, for calibration, so the certified function is the deployed one. Its constructor refuses a rejected specification, a stage without an implementation, and a checked obligation without a predicate. \section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}
% =====================================================================

Our claims are of three kinds, and each needs its own attack. Claims about the calculus are proved, so we test whether the proofs depend on the definitions they appear to use (E1, E2), by mutating those definitions. Claims about the implementation are tested by comparison with a reference interpreter and a model search written from the equations; these share an author with the checker, so they catch implementation errors and not a shared misreading of the semantics, which the proofs and the adequacy tests must cover. Exhaustive testing on small inputs relies on the small-scope hypothesis~\cite{jackson2019alloy}, and we add random testing of larger inputs and measure the oracle by mutation~\cite{jia2011analysis}, following the differential validation of executable semantics~\cite{wang2023kst}. Monolithic encodings in Z3 and PRISM~\cite{kwiatkowska2011prism} give a second derivation of each verdict, again by the same author from the same figure. Scale is measured on synthetic families built to stress each term of Theorem~\ref{thm:complexity}, including rejecting and exponential cases, as is usual for compositional analyses~\cite{bagheri2015covert}. Relevance cannot be shown by any of this, so we mine public configurations for what real stacks look like, and we apply one encoding rule to published designs. We use no LLM and no user study, so the usefulness of the output to practitioners is an open question.

\textbf{E15.} We analyzed the \resCorpusConfigs{} example configurations in the NeMo Guardrails repository at one commit, including Colang 2 flows and agent hook rails, and classified rails by name, with rules given in the artifact. \resCorpusWithRails{} configurations have real rails, using \resCorpusFlows{} distinct flows. Of the rails, \resCorpusLlm{} are prompted LLM tasks, \resCorpusService{} other models or external services, \resCorpusDeterministic{} deterministic, and \resCorpusTransforming{} rewrite their input, which our language cannot express. \resCorpusAllStat{} configurations are entirely statistical. \resCorpusAllDet{} are entirely deterministic, including regex rails for coding-agent hooks, and \resCorpusTool{} guard tool calls. So these configurations are short chains, mostly of learned stages, on text and not on actions. For such a chain the graph analysis of this paper is trivial, and the output of interest is its list of assumptions. Examples are written to teach a feature, so a stage count says little about production, and the corpus is one framework. It gives no evidence that escalation hierarchies are common; these appear in research designs and in our example. No configuration in it provides facts in our sense, so the results of Section~\ref{sec:complexity} on flow have no observed instance here.

\subsection{Failures, Deviations and Continuity}
\label{sec:failures}

Reviews of earlier versions of this study found defects, recorded in the artifact's errata with the first results. In brief: a reported measurement had been a constant; the contract-satisfaction code had not been executed by any experiment; the fingerprint omitted checked predicates and closures, so a stale certificate could stay open; the first statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:risk} was false; assumption lists omitted provided facts and veto stages that matter only through their facts; a first corpus extraction lost the Colang 2 configurations; and the PRISM model had the crediting rule built in, which also made it seem to explode. All are fixed and the experiments rerun. Our own attempt to prove a generalized meet lemma found it false, which led to Theorem~\ref{thm:complexity}. Earlier, E9 had shown a quadratic cost in the number of goals, since removed. Deviations from the protocol are marked in Table~\ref{tab:exps}; E12's literal rule failed on two laws in the first run and was relaxed afterwards.

An earlier preregistered scripted study of one fixed pipeline remains in the artifact. Its certificate requirement refused deployment in all \resPrimarySeeds{} primary seeds, which exposed a floating-point defect in a scripted scorer; any acceptance test on joint risk would have done the same. After the fix, certification changed no decision. A simple provenance rule reached lower risk than the full pipeline, because the sandbox could not exercise semantic guards, and no semantic mechanism resisted a distribution shift that the residual list had predicted. These results stand. They are consistent with the preference for deterministic establishment that Theorem~\ref{thm:order} and Table~\ref{tab:attackers} express, and they give no evidence that semantic guards work.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Internal.} One author wrote the calculus, checker, reference, encodings and specifications, so oracles share assumptions; the proofs tie the reference to the equations only on sampled terms. The script applying induction is unchecked. An obligation could be misstated and still pass, although mutants and adequacy tests reduce that risk. The runs were made on one day from uncommitted code. Hand classification of mutants and the addition of unit tests after seeing survivors bias E5. The mutation operators are a small set.

\textbf{Construct.} ``Safe'' is relative to declared goals and contracts: weak goals are easily safe, a false contract voids the guarantee, and an assumed obligation can be written down to silence a rejection. Safety says nothing about utility: the calculus would accept a hierarchy that denies nearly everything. Per-decision risk is not session harm. A joint bound can look small because attacks are rare.

\textbf{External.} The corpus is one framework's teaching examples. The designs were modelled by one person from publications, with no check by their authors and no second modeller. The language cannot express rewriting or voting, and the synthetic families show structure and not typical deployments. Timings come from one machine and a Python implementation, and PRISM's symbolic engines were unavailable. Nothing here involves a practitioner.

\textbf{Conclusion.} In E12 and E12b coverage holds up to Monte Carlo error; E12b's laws are small and its generator ours. Node slopes sit near the protocol's limit, and the theorem rather than the fit is the argument for near-linearity.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================

\textbf{Compositional verification in software engineering.} Our checker is modular verification over a loop-free program, in the tradition of verification conditions for passive programs~\cite{barnett2005weakest}, with atomic facts in place of formulas, and its core is available-expressions analysis~\cite{kildall1973unified,kam1977monotone}. What is specific is the domain model: adversarial stages, conditional contracts and evidence scoped to an invocation. COVERT also finds composition failures that no single component shows, by analyzing inter-app permission leakage compositionally~\cite{bagheri2015covert}. Chaki et al.\ verify across intellectual-property boundaries, where components are known only by their promises~\cite{chaki2013verification}, a situation close to that of third-party guards. He et al.\ learn assumptions for compositional verification of probabilistic models~\cite{he2016learning}. Learning obligations rather than declaring them is a natural direction for us. Su et al.\ bound how model checking results move with estimated parameters~\cite{su2016asymptotic}, which relates to drift. Differential validation of executable semantics~\cite{wang2023kst} is our model for conformance. Incremental recertification resembles build caching and regression test selection, and incremental product-line verification~\cite{kroher2022incremental}; prefix fingerprints are an application of that idea to statistical evidence.

\textbf{Contracts.} Assume-guarantee reasoning~\cite{misra1981proofs,jones1983tentative,abadi1993composing} and contract-based design~\cite{benveniste2018contracts,meyer1992applying} are our foundations, and we use a small fragment. Stochastic contracts~\cite{delahaye2011probabilistic,nuzzo2019stochastic} give richer algebras. A position paper proposes layers whose probabilistic guarantee discharges the next layer's assumption, with system bounds by the chain rule~\cite{arxiv260518672}. We take a different route: learned stages are measured at the level of a whole route, on the deployed function, so that no probabilistic guarantee is composed. This gives up per-layer modularity for statistical goals and avoids the conditional-independence questions of chaining. Work on redundancy and diversity in security~\cite{littlewood2004redundancy} treats the related question of what independent layers buy.

\textbf{Runtime enforcement for agents.} AgentSpec specifies runtime rules~\cite{arxiv250318666}, ContrAgent compiles assume-guarantee temporal contracts into monitors~\cite{arxiv260918128}, and behavioral contracts~\cite{arxiv260222302}, ShieldAgent~\cite{arxiv250322738}, VeriGuard~\cite{arxiv251005156}, ProbGuard~\cite{arxiv250800500} and NaSh~\cite{arxiv250613028} enforce or verify agent policies. These handle richer temporal properties of agent behavior, and several run on real agents, which we do not. Our question is the joint meaning of a stack of mechanisms. Shielding~\cite{arxiv150102573,arxiv170808611} and the Simplex architecture~\cite{sha2001using} are ancestors of the monitor-first design.

\textbf{Provenance and isolation.} CaMeL~\cite{arxiv250318813}, Fides~\cite{arxiv250523643} and the dual-LLM pattern~\cite{willison2023dual} give guarantees by construction, on real benchmarks in the first two cases. Our calculus expresses their structure, and a provided fact is the analogue of what they achieve, a deterministic mechanism establishing a property that later stages can use, although our model of CaMeL does not use one.

\textbf{Cascades and statistical guarantees.} Trust or Escalate~\cite{arxiv240718370} and Conformal Arbitrage~\cite{arxiv250600911} calibrate specific cascades, conformal methods control loss for LLM outputs~\cite{arxiv220802814,arxiv250220285,arxiv240210978}, and model cascades trade cost and quality~\cite{arxiv230505176}. These supply evidence for a route. ScenicProver composes probabilistic evidence for learning-enabled systems~\cite{arxiv251102164}.

\textbf{Guardrail frameworks and benchmarks.} LlamaFirewall~\cite{arxiv250503574} and NeMo Guardrails~\cite{arxiv231010501} are the systems whose configurations and designs we analyze. Benchmarks~\cite{arxiv240613352,arxiv241002644,arxiv241009024} measure effectiveness, which our results do not address and do not replace. \section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

We gave guardrail hierarchies a calculus, in which guards are bound only by conditional contracts, evidence belongs to one invocation, and a credited gate can provide facts to later stages. The composition check is sound and, relative to free stages, complete. Transitive reasoning about evidence is unsound. Gates belong before guards. Without flow of facts the check is near-linear, and with flow or correlated conditions the exact problem is coNP-hard, with a sound fast analysis and an exact refinement in the first case. Risk is a sum over routes, certificates are bound to route prefixes, and a change invalidates only what it precedes.

The evidence covers the composition and not its parts. In the public example configurations we studied, stacks are short chains, and there the value of the approach is a list of assumptions and claims, rather than the graph analysis. Whether that list helps engineers is unknown. Next steps are to port the proofs to a proof assistant, to add rewriting stages and session properties, to run the method with practitioners and a second modeller, and to discharge contracts for real guards.
