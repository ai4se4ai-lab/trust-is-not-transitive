## Findings by severity

### Critical

**F1. The "exposed variant" is misdescribed, and its witness is not $T_j^p$.**
*Location:* Sec. `sec:worked` ("The exposed variant"); `devagent_exposed_judge.json`; Table `tab:cases`.

*Problem.* The text says: "Now remove gate $c$, so that all input reaches the judge." That is false. The file keeps switch $s$ on `input_tainted`, and tainted input still goes to $v_2$ and then to the person. Only the untainted arm reaches $n_3$.

The paper then says the witness "is $T_j^p$ reconstructed mechanically". The same section says "On $T_j^p$ the patch is tainted and takes the same path" to the person. In the variant $T_j^p$ still takes that path. The witness run has $s$ take the untainted branch and then violate $\varphi_3$, the goal about untrusted content. It therefore exists only because switches are free in the semantics.

So the rejection says "a switch gives no credit, so the taint test must be repeated as a gate". It does not reproduce the attack of the introduction. A careful reader will find this at once, and it is the paper's central illustration.

*Fix.* Make the exposed variant what the introduction describes: delete both $s$ and $c$, or send both arms to $n_3$. Regenerate the finding and the witness; I expect the same obligation finding, with the path through no switch. If the present variant is kept, present it as a lesson about switches, and drop "all input reaches the judge" and "This is $T_j^p$".

**F2. In the redesigned DevAgent no learned stage does any work for $\varphi_3$, and the person who does it has an unexamined contract.**
*Location:* Thm. `thm:ni` closing paragraph; Fig. `fig:devagent`; Sec. `sec:worked`; Table `tab:catalog`; `devagent.json`.

*Problem.* This is the question I was asked about, and my answer is that the design is logically tidy but not credible as an example of a guardrail hierarchy. There are five separate points.

1. **The judge is idle.** $\varphi_3$ is "never act on instructions embedded in untrusted content". Gate $c$ verifies that no untrusted text is present. On that arm $\varphi_3$ holds with no judge at all, yet $c$ has `establishes: null`. I set `establishes: phi3` at $c$: the spec is accepted and the residual list becomes empty. Replacing $n_3$ by a release guard of empty scope gives the same result. The paper's single residual entry, `miss_rate_bounded` at $n_3$, "which removal shows to be necessary", is thus an artifact of how $c$ was declared. Round 7's Finding 2 has moved rather than gone: the judge reads text only where there is nothing to detect.
2. **The fact is misnamed.** Gate $c$ provides `contract_holds_on_adversarial_text`, while what it verifies is that no adversarial text exists. The catalog says "an obligation that the input is clean makes no sense for a detector of unclean input". Gate $c$ supplies that obligation to $L_3$ under another name. The vacuous truth is valid in the logic, but the name hides what happened.
3. **The human contradicts the paper's own argument.** The text says a judge reading the structured view "cannot decide $\varphi_3$ on a patch, because a malicious and a benign patch can have equal views". `L4` is `view: structured` with `scope: [phi3]`, so the person's contract fails by the same argument. If the person reads the patch to decide, then the view is raw, and "a guard that must judge content … can be addressed by it" applies to the person. I set `L4` to raw and the adversarial check rejects at $n_4$. "Neither arm claims that a guard can read attacker text safely" holds only by the view label. In short: DevAgent passes because the one stage that must read attacker-influenced content is declared not to.
4. **The hardest assumption is off the list.** The person is taken to decide $\varphi_3$ exactly, given `human_sees_provenance`, which has kind `construction`. So it is not on the residual list, and Table `tab:cases` says DevAgent "rests on: miss_rate_bounded". Yet the paper's final value claim is that this list shows what an assurance argument rests on. The catalog names "attention under load", but no such obligation appears in the case. The literature is plain on this: approval fatigue and automation bias are why CaMeL treats user prompts as a cost. Table `tab:attackers` has no row or column for a person under adaptive social engineering.
5. **The workload is unmeasured, and for an issue-triage agent the tainted arm is nearly everything.** Public issues, external pull requests and third-party dependencies are all untrusted, and anything an LLM derives from them should be labelled too. So nearly all consequential work goes to a person, and the agent is a suggestion tool. $T_b$ shows this and the paper admits it in one clause ("the cost of the design"). In addition, a scorer retrained on route $n_4$ "costs one route", but that route's certificate requires $N$ runs of the deployed function, which on this route includes the person, so about 1800 human decisions per recalibration on the paper's own numbers. Calibrating a human's error rate is not discussed. Removing $v_2$ altogether leaves the verdict and the residual unchanged, so the scorer's role (triage load) is invisible to the analysis.

*Credit where due.* The structural idea is sound. Provenance split plus deterministic establishment is what the good literature recommends, and the calculus now expresses it. The right conclusion is a stronger one than the paper draws: on the evidence here, the safe design for semantic goals is to have no learned stage on the route. Say that.

*Fix.* Choose one of two.

- (a) Let $c$ establish $\varphi_3$, remove the judge's claim on that goal, and give $L_3$ a goal it can actually serve on clean input, for example `task_appropriate` ("the action fits the request") as a statistical goal with an honest residual. Keep the raw judge there for what it does well.
- (b) Keep a judge on the tainted arm with an open, stated claim on robustness, listed as a residual. This shows the tool reporting the real risk, instead of designing it away.

In either case:

- Set `L4` to a view that shows content, add `reviewer_attention` (kind assumed) and a per-reviewer load limit, and make it a residual entry.
- Add a person row to Table `tab:attackers`.
- Add a taint-completeness premise to Table `tab:assumptions` (LLM-derived values must inherit labels; a false negative means the raw judge meets attacker text). Today "honest label store" covers honesty and not completeness.
- State how route $n_4$ is certified, or exempt human routes with a reason.
- Add at least a back-of-envelope estimate of the share of traffic that reaches a person.

**F3. The E15 conclusion is not supported, because the corpus misses most of what matters.**
*Location:* Sec. `sec:rq4` E15; `corpus_analysis.py`; `corpus/nemo_rails.json`; `e15_corpus.json`; abstract; Conclusion; RQ3 ("Real hierarchies are small (E15)").

*Problem.* The paper is careful about generalising beyond one framework. The trouble is that the numbers are wrong even inside that framework.

1. **All Colang 2.x configurations are lost.** 72 of 139 rows have `colang_version: 2.x`, and every one is among the 93 rows with no rails recorded. In Colang 2.x rails can be written in `.co` files, not YAML. Example: `examples/configs/nemoguards_v2/` has no flows in `config.yml`, while its `rails.co` runs content safety, topic safety and jailbreak detection on input and content safety on output. That is four stages, counted as zero. Its v1 twin `nemoguards` is counted with four. The rows named `ai_defense_v2`, `clavata_v2`, `crowdstrike_aidr_v2`, `f5_guardrails_v2`, `pangea_v2`, `sensitive_data_detection_v2` and `trend_micro_v2` are probably the same case, though I checked only the first example. Text saying "\resCorpusWithRails{} have real rails" invites the reading that the rest have none.
2. **The configurations closest to the paper's subject are lost.** `examples/configs/agent_hooks/claude_code/config.yml` uses `rails.tool_call.per_tool` with regex checks on `Bash`, `Read`, `Write` and `Edit`, an allowlist of tools, and `models: []`. That is, a coding-agent stack of deterministic stages with no model call. Its corpus row has `tool_call: None`, as do the `codex` and `nemo_relay` rows, because the extractor reads a list and these are mappings. So "\resCorpusTool{} has rails on tool calls" should be at least 4, and those omitted are the opposite of "statistical". `injection_detection` is recorded with only a `config` entry and is treated as railless, although its config enables injection rules that reject. How it is wired needs checking.
3. **"None escalates" is not measured.** `'escalating': 0` is a literal in `corpus_analysis.py`. The errata and Sec. `sec:failures` record that "a reported measurement had been a constant". This is the same defect, in a paper that says all results are generated. It is also tautological: the framework has no escalate verdict for these rails, so the sample could not show otherwise. Selecting a framework that cannot express the pattern says nothing about the pattern. The sentence "It does not support the claim that escalation hierarchies are common" is right but too weak; the corpus could not have supported or refuted it.
4. **`parallel: 0` is not measured either.** The code looks for keys ending in `.parallel`, and the corpus has no such keys, so the field cannot be anything but zero. NeMo does document `rails.input.parallel` (default false). So "rails in this framework run in sequence" is inaccurate as a general statement. The docs also warn that mutating rails in parallel can diverge, which is relevant to Table `tab:coverage`.
5. **The reported check does not match the sentence, and it adds nothing.** The text says "with no bound supplied, \resCorpusOpen{} of \resCorpusWithRails{} leave a goal open". The code supplies `miss_rate_bounded` and withholds the robustness obligation, and the key is `open_under_adversary`. In either form, the number is just the count of configurations containing at least one non-deterministic stage. The analysis derives nothing that the classifier did not put in.
6. **The denominator is wrong.** I checked the four "closed" configurations. Three are `gliner`, `polygraf` and `privateai` masking-only configs. Their stages are dropped as inexpressible, so they are skipped, and they count as not open. Only `tool_safety_check` is genuinely closed. The figure should be 39 of 40 analysable, with 3 not expressible.
7. **Classification by substring is unvalidated.** Flows that match nothing become "model or service" by default, and this bucket is 40 of the 86 stages. It may contain deterministic or pattern stages. Examples worth checking are the competitor check and `detect pii`, which in some setups is pattern-based. `jailbreak detection heuristics` is called deterministic although, as I recall, its checks use a language model's perplexity; and it is not exact on any goal, so by the paper's own rule it is not a deterministic gate. The two "tool validation" names need their `.co` bodies read. The paper says "the classification is ours", but no rail implementation was read, no second rater was used, and there is no agreement figure.
8. **The sample measures documentation, not practice.** Most flows occur once: 57 distinct flows across 86 stages. Each vendor integration example shows its own rail, usually one input and one output. The median of 2 is thus how a tutorial is written. The largest (9) is a sample configuration containing placeholder rails. "Short" is not evidence of what deployments do. The rails are also mostly on chat input and output, so for a paper on tool-using agents the sample scarcely applies.
9. **Not reproducible.** The extraction script is not in the repository. Only its output is. The commit is fixed, which is good, but a reader cannot see or rerun what was dropped. All 138 readable rows have `tools: False`, so this field gives no information.

*Fix.*

1. Commit the extractor, and parse `.co` files and `per_tool` mappings.
2. Compute `escalating` and `parallel` from the data or delete them. State which are impossible by construction.
3. Report the funnel: total, no guardrail intent, YAML rails, Colang rails, hook rails, not expressible.
4. Read each distinct flow's implementation and classify by mechanism, with a second rater and kappa.
5. Report the stack on tool actions separately.
6. Add at least one framework that can express escalation. Permission systems of coding agents (allow, ask, deny) are the obvious case, since they are rule stages escalating to a person. That is DevAgent's shape, and this data may well rescue the paper's premise. Guardrails AI on-fail policies (reask, fix, exception) and LangGraph or similar interrupt nodes are also candidates.
7. Remove "Real hierarchies are small (E15)" until the numbers stand.

### Major

**F4. Verdicts still turn on the modeller, now through `view` and through the bound/measurement split.**
*Location:* Sec. `sec:practice` ("These can be applied mechanically"); E13; Table `tab:cases`; `run.adversarial`.

*Problem.* The uniform rule adds the robustness obligation only for `view == 'raw'`, so whoever writes the view decides. I confirmed this:

| Change | Original adversarial verdict | New verdict |
|---|---|---|
| DevAgent: `L4` to raw | accepted | rejected at `n4` |
| Policy classes: `Judge` to raw | accepted | rejected at `j` |
| CaMeL-style: `Policy` to raw | accepted | rejected at `run` |

The sharpest case is the row "Policy classes (expressiveness)". Its judge has `view: structured` and `scope: [semantic_injection]`, and it is accepted under the adversary. By the paragraph after Thm. `thm:ni`, a judge on that view cannot decide such a goal. So the rule exempts exactly the stage that is unable to do its job. That row is the old design the previous round objected to, and it stands in the table with the same verdict as the fixed one, unexplained in the text. Table `tab:cases` has no column showing it.

*Fix.* Add a second uniform rule: a stage may not have a goal in scope unless the goal is decidable from its view, argued in a short note per stage. A "semantic" goal then needs a content view, and so needs the robustness claim. Remove the "Policy classes" row or explain it and mark it as failing that rule. Drop "mechanically", or say that it applies given the views. Show the views in the table.

**F5. A bounded miss rate is treated as a per-proposal fact, and measurement is treated inconsistently.**
*Location:* Sec. `sec:practice`; E13 text; Table `tab:catalog`; Fig. `fig:semantics`.

*Problem.* Two connected issues.

- In Fig. `fig:semantics`, a credited gate gives $\varphi\in S$ and an accepting guard gives its scope, categorically, on this proposal. So `miss_rate_bounded` in $E(n)$ means "this bound exists, therefore this stage did not miss here". That is false for every bound above zero. Round 7 raised this for Trust or Escalate only (Finding 7); the uniform rule now makes it universal. Sec. `sec:risk` says "learned stages do not meet exact contracts" and treats statistical goals separately, but Secs. `sec:practice` and `sec:rq4` read as if the checker verifies something for those stages. In fact on statistical goals the obligation is a to-do marker with a theorem-shaped reading. A reader needs this said in one sentence.
- Table `tab:catalog` says the static analyzer's `miss_rate_bounded` is "discharged by measured recall on the code base". E13 says NeMo and LlamaFirewall "state measurements, not bounds", and therefore their goals are open even with a benign threat model. Measured counts give a Clopper-Pearson bound in a line, conditional on the test mix. Trust or Escalate's guarantee is a different quantity (conditional agreement) yet counts as `miss_rate_bounded`. So LlamaFirewall "rejected, benign" against Trust or Escalate "accepted, benign" depends on the literary form of the paper and on a name that covers unlike quantities.

*Fix.* In contracts, name the obligation for what it does: "stage's statistical claim is accounted for by a route certificate". State that for goals in $\Goals_s$ the check gives accounting and not safety. For E13, compute bounds from published counts where possible and report them, and say what each bound conditions on. Consider replacing the benign column with "measurement stated: yes/no; distribution: …".

**F6. The abstract's last sentence is not honest, though the rest is.**
*Location:* abstract.

*Problem.* The abstract improves greatly: "The approach does not show that any guard meets its contract" is exactly the right sentence. Three problems remain.

- "An analysis of public configurations shows that today's stacks are short chains of statistical stages." The body says "one framework's examples and not a sample of production systems". A one-framework sample of tutorials cannot show what "today's stacks" are, and F3 shows the numbers are wrong too. For coding-agent hooks in the same repository, the statement is false.
- "for which that list of assumptions is the main result." For veto chains under the uniform rule the list is stage × two obligations, as the identical NeMo and LlamaFirewall rows of Table `tab:cases` show. No analysis is needed, and the paper elsewhere admits "a team with a two-stage stack could write it by hand". The abstract thus closes on a benefit the body doubts.
- "an independent model search": Sec. `sec:eval` correctly says the oracles share an author and catch implementation errors only. "Independent" should go. The abstract also never says plainly that no LLM, attack or practitioner was involved.

At 299 words, it also exceeds the 250 words I recall from IEEE's guidance. Check this.

*Fix.* Cut the final two sentences to: "In one framework's public examples, stacks had few stages and rarely reached tool calls; there, structure adds little, and the method's use is to state assumptions. We ran no LLM, no attack and no practitioner study." Adjust after F3. Replace "independent" by "a separately written". Cut the sentence on proof hardening.

**F7. The value proposition is thin where stacks are simple, and the paper has no evidence for the case where they are not.**
*Location:* Secs. `sec:eval`, `sec:conclusion`.

*Problem.* The paper reports that the graph analysis is trivial for what it found, that the assumption list can be written by hand, that nothing was evaluated with practitioners, and that no guard was run. All true and to its credit. An editor will then ask for whom the method is. The only hierarchy in the paper where it changes anything is the author's own, which has F1 and F2. I see no case in which the tool found something an attentive engineer would have missed.

*Fix.* The strongest available step is a real end-to-end case. Translate one real tool-using configuration (for example a coding agent's permission rules plus a hook) to \HSL by script, run the check, and report one finding the maintainers confirm. The repository has an `src/tint/bench` Ollama scorer and judge from earlier work. A small run with a real local judge on the DevAgent arms, even with few attacks, would give the paper its first contact with a real guard, and could quantify the idle-judge question. If no such case can be produced, the paper should present itself as a foundations paper and not as a method for practitioners.

**F9. What a TSE reader still lacks at 15 pages.**
*Location:* whole paper. The target is 15 to 25 pages, so there is room.

The practice section is useful. The catalog, coverage table and premises table are what I would give a team; "Choose goals independently of tools" and "make each assumed obligation someone's job" are good advice. Weak points of that section: it is entirely unevaluated (the paper says so), the "In a Development Process" subsection is one dense paragraph, and the example does not follow it (the person has no owner or attention obligation, F2).

Missing, in order of value:

1. Real case end to end (F7), about 2 pages.
2. A corrected corpus with an escalation-capable framework (F3), about 1.5 pages.
3. A threat-model section. Attacker capabilities now sit in one table and scattered clauses. Readers from security expect the attacker's reach (which inputs, which tools, label store, memory) in one place. Session-level attacks in which each proposal looks safe alone are mentioned only as "per-decision risk is not session harm".
4. Human approval in depth (about 0.5 page). Volume, what is displayed, fatigue, and how it enters risk.
5. A rewriting extension or an explicit treatment. Six masking stages in the corpus are dropped. A guard's input can also be sanitized, which is the most common practical response to injection, and the paper's own coverage table calls it the first extension. Even a sketched transform stage with a contract on output would be worthwhile.
6. A short list of finding types with responses (a "what to do when the checker says X" table), to replace the prose of the process subsection.
7. Benign reachability (round 7, Finding 5). One dataflow fact would stop `deny` being a passing answer, and it would catch a design with an empty judge arm.
8. An example of a certificate for DevAgent with a configuration and a stale event. The current numbers are generic.
