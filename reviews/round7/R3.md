# Reviewer 3 report: "Trust Is Not Transitive: A Sound, Complete and Scalable Verification Approach for Hierarchical LLM Guardrails"

Venue: IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering. Round 7.
Focus: relevance and presentation.

## Scores (1 = poor, 5 = excellent)

| Criterion | Score | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Novelty | 3 | The evidence-scoped semantics and route-level certificates are new. The decision procedure is a small must-analysis, and a monolithic Z3 encoding reaches the same verdict in 96 ms at depth 256. |
| Rigor | 3 | The calculus and its conformance evidence are strong. RQ4 is weak: its verdicts are set by contracts the author chose. The statistical layer and the structural layer are less connected than the text implies. |
| Relevance | 2 | Against an adversary the guarantee reduces to deterministic gates plus assumed contracts. The running example's fix removes the judge's ability to judge. There is no evidence that real stacks have the assumed shape. |
| Verifiability and Transparency | 3 | The paper is candid about failures, and every number is generated. However, the protocol and cases are uncommitted, the case models changed after first results, and the earlier study has no citation. |
| Presentation | 3 | The structure is clear and much of the prose is good. The paper has no worked example, no HSL listing, no contract-writing guidance, and a rhetorical style that recurs. |

## Recommendation

**Major revision.** The formal core is worth publishing. The paper does not yet show that it answers a question practitioners have, and its one contact with real systems (E13) does not measure what it claims to.

## Basis of this review

- I read the body of `paper/main.tex`, all eight files in `experiments/v2/cases/`, `experiments/v2/PROTOCOL.md`, `docs/ERRATA.md`, the bibliography, and the relevant parts of `src/tint/hsl/`.
- I read the arXiv full texts of NeMo Guardrails, LlamaFirewall, CaMeL, Trust or Escalate, Conformal Arbitrage and AgentSpec. For ContrAgent and the layered position paper I read only the abstracts.
- Other citations are judged from titles and my own knowledge, and are marked as such.

## Findings, ranked by severity

### Critical

**1. The E13 verdicts are set by the modeller's inputs, not found by the analysis.**
*Location:* Sec. RQ4 (paragraph "Three observations follow"), Table `tab:cases`, `experiments/v2/cases/*.json`.

*What is wrong.* The cases differ in two things the author chose: which obligation each layer requires, and whether it is placed in `evidence`.

- Trust or Escalate, Conformal Arbitrage and DevAgent's raw scorer `L2` require `iid_calibration`, and it is supplied.
- NeMo's rails and LlamaFirewall's stages require invented obligations (`input_free_of_addressed_instructions`, `miss_rate_certified`, `trace_free_of_addressed_instructions`), and `evidence` is left empty.

The check then returns what was written in. I confirmed this on scratch copies:

| Change to the case file | Original verdict | New verdict |
|---|---|---|
| LlamaFirewall: add the two existing names to `evidence` | rejected | **accepted**, 2 residual |
| NeMo: add the existing name to `evidence` on both rails | rejected | **accepted**, 2 residual |
| Trust or Escalate: give the raw judges NeMo's obligation, unsupplied | accepted | **rejected** |
| DevAgent: give raw scorer `L2` NeMo's obligation, unsupplied | accepted | **rejected** at `n2` |

So every accepted/rejected split in the table flips under a one-line change, and each change is as defensible as the original. All of these stages read raw attacker-reachable text. Nothing in the paper justifies treating a text scorer differently from a moderation rail. The paper itself cites scorer evasion in Background.

The closing sentence, "the published designs with the strongest structural guarantees are the ones it accepts", is circular. It also fails on the facts: Trust or Escalate and Conformal Arbitrage have no adversary in their threat models (Finding 7).

`docs/ERRATA.md` calls the first-run accepted verdicts for NeMo and LlamaFirewall "artefacts of the specification". The corrected rejections are artefacts in the same sense. The paper's disclaimer ("our models, not those of the designs' authors") does not remove this.

*Fix.*
1. Delete the "strongest structural guarantees" claim.
2. State one rule for assigning contracts before modelling, by mechanism and not by paper. For example: every stage reading attacker-reachable text carries the same injection obligation. Apply it uniformly, including to `L2`.
3. Report verdict sensitivity. For each case give the minimal evidence set that flips the verdict; this is the useful output.
4. Have an independent modeller, ideally someone from those teams, write the specifications, and report agreement.

**2. The running example's remedy removes the judge's ability to judge, so its contract cannot be met.**
*Location:* Introduction (traces, contract); Sec. Specification Language; Thm. `thm:ni` and the paragraph after it; `Engine.view_for`.

*What is wrong.* The whole narrative is that $T_j^p$, "a patch that breaks no deterministic rule, so only the judge stands between it and the repository", is resolved by giving the judge a structured view. In the artifact that view is six booleans (`destructive`, `production`, `approved`, `external`, `secret`, `untrusted`) plus the scorer's number. The judge never sees the patch.

Apply Theorem `thm:ni` to two patches, one malicious and one benign, with equal booleans and equal score. Their verdicts must be equal. The contract "I block unsafe actions" can therefore hold only if the score alone separates them, in which case `L3` adds nothing to `L2`. The boolean inputs are already decided by gates, so `L3` is a lookup table on a number. An LLM has no role.

More generally, $\varphi_3$ ("never act on instructions embedded in untrusted content") is a property of the relation between content and action. A guard denied the content cannot decide it. The paper never faces the central trade-off in this area: guards need to read the text that can subvert them. The dual-LLM pattern and CaMeL escape it by not asking a guard to judge semantics. DevAgent asks exactly that.

The sentence "A guard declared with a structured view reads no free text, so text alone cannot change its verdict" is true, but read alongside the contract it undercuts the example.

*Fix.* Either (a) restate DevAgent so that $L_3$ decides something the view can support, and say plainly that $\varphi_3$ on patches stays unverified; or (b) define a richer view (AST facts, taint-labelled diff, tool schema) and state which attacker-controlled strings remain, such as identifiers, paths and comments. Add a short result or discussion making the view/capability tension explicit: a guard's contract can only depend on what distinguishes its view.

**3. The claim about "adversaries with contracts" assumes the hard part away, and the guarantee left over is modest.**
*Location:* Sec. Semantics ("Safety quantifies over all behaviours... It therefore covers attackers that adapt across proposals"); Sec. `sec:limits`; abstract.

*What is wrong.* The quantifier is over guards that keep their contracts. For a statistical guard, an adaptive attacker is exactly what breaks the contract. "Covers adaptive attackers, provided the contracts hold" is therefore close to vacuous for the threat that motivates the paper. Contracts have the form "on acceptance, under obligations, the goal holds", which means zero false negatives. The paper concedes this: "an LLM judge's contract cannot honestly be taken as exact".

Following that through:

- Theorem `thm:sound` gives a usable guarantee only for goals established by deterministic gates, and for assumed obligations someone takes on faith.
- Theorem `thm:certified` holds under i.i.d. draws from $D$. `sec:limits` says "bounds on $R$ are no defence against adaptation".
- So against the adversary, the assurance is: every path has deterministic gates for the hard goals. That is true and useful, but it is the old reference-monitor argument in the paper's own Background.

The structural and statistical parts are also weakly connected. The empirical bound on $R_r$ does not use contracts for $\Goals_s$; Theorem `thm:risk` explicitly exempts them. A raw-text judge rejected for a missing `structured_view` would receive an equally valid Clopper-Pearson bound under $D$. The headline defect of the introduction thus has no effect on the headline statistical quantity. The paper should say so.

The strongest honest statement is this: "the checker tells you which claims in your assurance argument rest on what". That is valuable. "Sound and complete verification of guardrails" oversells it to a reader who skims.

*Fix.*
1. Add a table near the front: attacker class (benign drift, static attack set, adaptive) against what is guaranteed, under which assumptions.
2. Delete or qualify "covers attackers that adapt".
3. State explicitly that the composition check does not affect the certificate for statistical goals, or show how it does. For example, split $D$ into strata, or make the obligation enter the calibration requirement.
4. In title and abstract, say what is verified: that the declared contracts entail the goals. Consider dropping "Sound, Complete and Scalable" from the title.

**4. The premise that real stacks are escalation hierarchies has no evidence, and "no corpus exists" is not an adequate answer.**
*Location:* Introduction first paragraph; Sec. Evaluation ("since no corpus of industrial hierarchies exists"); threats (External).

*What is wrong.* The introduction describes "fast rules first, then classifiers, then an LLM judge or a person for what remains" and cites LlamaFirewall and NeMo. Neither is that.

- NeMo organises rails by lifecycle stage and describes moderation as a pipeline.
- LlamaFirewall's PromptGuard does give "early, low-cost filtering" before AlignmentCheck. However, its evaluation scans different message roles independently and flags a trace if either scanner fires "at any point". An attack that first succeeds and is flagged later counts as defended.
- The paper's own models of both are veto chains with no escalation.

Hence the escalation pattern that creates the core defect appears only in two academic calibration papers and in the author's invented DevAgent. If real stacks are veto chains of two to five stages, the analysis reduces to a check an engineer can do by eye, and the threat section admits that a million nodes is "headroom rather than need". An editor will ask what the tool adds.

*Fix.* Build the corpus. Public NeMo `config.yml` files, Guardrails AI configurations, LangChain or LlamaIndex agent middleware, agent-framework guardrail hooks and permission settings in open-source coding agents can all be mined. Report the shapes found: depth, escalation frequency, routes, stage types, unsupported constructs. Even 30 to 50 real configurations would replace speculation with data and would let you report how many latent findings exist. A case study with one team who act on a finding would be stronger still.

### Major

**5. No utility or liveness notion: `deny` is safe.**
*Location:* Def. `def:safe`; Sec. Semantics ("A guard that escalates everything meets every contract"); intro trace $T_b$.

*What is wrong.* Safety is trivially satisfiable. The analysis does not distinguish a useful hierarchy from one that blocks everything. $T_b$, introduced to show that same-shaped actions can be legitimate, is never used again. The only utility data in the paper's history appears in macros and is not reported. The cited systems report this trade-off as their main result: LlamaFirewall reports ASR 17.6% to 1.75% with utility 47.7% to 42.7%, and CaMeL treats human prompting and fatigue as a design concern.

*Fix.* Add a benign-reachability condition, such as: for each declared benign class, a contract-respecting path to acceptance exists. It costs one more dataflow fact and returns $T_b$ to the story. Add a discussion of false-positive cost, escalation load and approval fatigue.

**6. The calculus omits behaviours common in real guardrails, and "any acyclic hierarchy" overstates coverage.**
*Location:* Sec. Syntax; contribution 1; threats (External).

*What is wrong.* Unmodelled: rewriting and masking stages (the proposal must pass unchanged), reask or retry loops (mentioned only in threats), parallel or k-of-n voting, score aggregation, stateful session policies under certificates, and multi-way LLM routing. The human approver appears in the abstract's first sentence but is never given a contract, so a reader cannot tell how to model approval. Sanitization is the case where order matters. Note also that the Background cites edit automata, which exist to transform actions, as if they enforced only safety properties.

The switch is described as routing "on a trusted condition", yet $W$ is unconstrained in the semantics. Two switches on the same condition are treated as independent, so a safe hierarchy with correlated conditions is rejected. "Never a false alarm" holds only relative to free branch choice. State this limit next to the completeness theorem.

The introduction asks "Is the order of two layers significant?" and no result answers it. As modelled, gate order does not affect the verdict: `avail` is a union along the path. That answer is itself interesting, and it is a limit given that real orderings matter.

*Fix.* Give a coverage table: construct, expressible or not, encoding. Sketch a `transform` stage with a contract over the output, or state why it is out of scope. Answer the order question explicitly. Show how to model a human approver, with an obligation on what the human is shown.

**7. Trust or Escalate and Conformal Arbitrage are misdescribed as stage contracts and as a "positive pattern".**
*Location:* Sec. RQ4; `trust_or_escalate.json`, `conformal_arbitrage.json`.

*What is wrong.*

- **Trust or Escalate** concerns LLM *evaluation* of pairs of model generations, not guarding actions. Its guarantee is high-probability conditional agreement, $P(\text{agrees}\mid\text{LLM evaluates}) \ge 1-\alpha$. It has no adversary. Its thresholds are calibrated per judge, which fits per-route assumptions. Modelling the guarantee as the categorical promise `scope: human_agreement` turns a population rate into a per-proposal fact, and the conditional quantity is not the paper's joint risk. Items the cascade abstains on go unevaluated; the model's `deny` leaf loses this.
- **Conformal Arbitrage** learns "a single scalar threshold" with conformal risk control, and the guarantee is a bound on *expected* residual Guardian loss for the whole policy. It is not a per-stage contract. Hence "a calibration assumption per route" and "each stage has a stated conditional contract" are both wrong for it. The Guardian (possibly a human) chooses among candidate actions; it is not an accept/block guard. Its loss is defined by the Guardian's own scores, and the paper states no adversary that I could find.

Calling these "the positive pattern" alongside firewalls compares systems with incompatible threat models. They pass because no attacker is in scope.

*Fix.* Report them as out-of-threat-model examples of statistical composition. Add an obligation for exchangeability under attack. Correct the Conformal Arbitrage text and model it with one shared calibration event. Add a column for threat model to Table `tab:cases`.

**8. LlamaFirewall and NeMo models contain errors and contracts their authors would not recognise.**
*Location:* Sec. RQ4; `llamafirewall_composition.json`, `nemo_rails.json`.

*What is wrong.*

- **Self-defeating obligations.** `trace_free_of_addressed_instructions` is required for AlignmentCheck, whose stated purpose is to detect injection-induced misalignment. `input_free_of_addressed_instructions` is required for the input rail, which exists to detect jailbreaks. An obligation that the input is clean, on a detector of unclean input, makes the detector pointless. No such condition is stated in either source; the manuscript text says only that they state none, and does not say the condition is ours.
- **NeMo ordering.** The NeMo paper has input moderation act "before reaching the dialogue system". The case places `colang_flow` first. The verdict is unaffected, which points back to Finding 6, but the model is wrong as written.
- **Colang is not deterministic.** `colang_flow` is credited as a deterministic gate establishing `dialogue_flow`. The paper generates the user canonical form with the LLM by few-shot prompting, and the LLM decides next steps for undefined forms. Only the pre-defined flow step is deterministic. The hard credit is therefore unjustified under the paper's own definition.
- **CodeShield credited by a goal named after its output.** `known_insecure_pattern` is credited exactly, while LlamaFirewall reports 96% precision and 79% recall for identifying insecure code. A goal defined as "this tool found nothing" is tautological. This is the general problem with "deterministic gate establishes $\varphi$": the semantics takes exactness from the goal's definition.
- **Time and role structure are lost.** PromptGuard reads user and tool messages, AlignmentCheck reads assistant messages, and evaluation is incremental across a trace. A one-shot linear chain over one proposal is not that system.
- **Scope of comparison.** NeMo's case drops fact-checking, hallucination, topical and execution rails. The NeMo caution is about "our toolkit" and recommends pairing programmable with embedded rails. The manuscript attaches it to moderation rails and omits the recommendation. NeMo addresses conversational applications rather than tool-using coding agents, which should be said.

*Fix.* Correct the models. State which obligations were introduced by you. Report LlamaFirewall's own measured numbers alongside the finding, so readers can see what the structural analysis adds. Replace tool-output goals with properties independent of the tool, e.g. "no injectable SQL in emitted code", and mark the gate as a veto with a recall obligation.

**9. The CaMeL description is largely right but omits residuals and does not meet the paper's own "hard route" definition.**
*Location:* Sec. RQ4; Related Work ("deterministic establishment makes routes hard"); `camel_style.json`.

*What is wrong.* Credit where due: "trusted query" is stated by CaMeL. But its stated assumptions and non-goals also include uncompromised memory, text-to-text attacks and phishing with no data-flow effect, and it does not aim at full autonomy without human intervention. It also discusses side channels. Policy adequacy is an obvious further dependency, though that is my reading and not a stated assumption. The model records one residual obligation, and the capability policy gate has none, although policies are authored artefacts and are the system's central point of failure. A failed policy may lead to asking the user, as the running examples there show, and no such path exists in the model. Planning is a generation step, not a pass/fail gate.

The `plan` gate has a layer, so `hsl.gated` does not count it and `control_flow_integrity` is not gate-established. Under the paper's definition this is a veto stage, not a deterministic gate. Related Work's sentence therefore does not hold for the case as written, unless that goal is in $\Goals_d$. The table does not show which.

*Fix.* Add `policy_adequate` and memory-integrity residuals. Include the approval path. State which goals are hard and report hard versus statistical routes in the table.

**10. Joint risk is the wrong quantity to headline for rare attacks, and no worked number is shown.**
*Location:* Sec. `sec:risk`; Thm. `thm:certified`.

*What is wrong.* $R=\Pr[Y=1\wedge\text{accepted}]$ scales with attack prevalence. If 0.1% of proposals are attacks, a guard that misses every attack has $R=0.001$ and, with an appropriately sized sample, certifies under a 1% budget. A deployer cannot read safety from such a certificate. The artifact has a macro for accepted-conditional risk (25.40% in the exploratory run), showing that the author knows of the gap, but the paper omits it. The budget $\varepsilon$ used is never stated in the body. Nothing discusses who labels $Y$, where $D$ comes from, or how many calibration attacks are realistic.

*Fix.* Report conditional risk on the unsafe population, or per attack class, next to joint risk, and show how each scales with prevalence. Add guidance for sample sizing. For $\delta_r=0.025$ and $N=1800$, zero observed losses give $u_r\approx0.20\%$ and ten give $\approx1.02\%$. Discuss labelling cost and the source of attack data.

**11. Incrementality is weak where it matters, and "closing" a route is an outage.**
*Location:* Sec. Incremental Recertification; E11.

*What is wrong.* In DevAgent, gates precede all routes. Changing any of the three rules or `L2` closes both routes. Only a change to `L3` leaves anything open. Rule updates are the most frequent change, so the common case gains nothing, and the 44% figure from random hierarchies says little. The intro's question about the retrained scorer has the answer "everything after it", which the paper never says for its own example.

A closed route denies, so the agent stops until fresh labelled data is drawn, the certificate is rebuilt, and the bound passes. The paper mentions no shadow mode or staged rollout. Also, fingerprints are conservative: by Theorem `thm:mono`, tightening a gate cannot increase $R_r$, so a certified monotone tightening need not stale anything.

*Fix.* Walk through DevAgent's change matrix (stage changed, routes stale). Add a monotone-change rule that keeps certificates valid when a change only tightens. Add a deployment path: shadow calibration against the new prefix, then atomic switch. Report on realistic shapes from the corpus (Finding 4).

**12. The positioning table is self-favouring, and the footnote does not repair it.**
*Location:* Table `tab:related`.

*What is wrong.* All five columns are this paper's own features, and the only row with all "Y" is the paper's. "Complete" and "Any DAG" exist as criteria only because of this work. The caption's disclaimer concedes the point while keeping the visual effect. "U" conflates "not applicable", "not claimed" and "unknown": a statistical guarantee is not a goal of CaMeL, and ContrAgent's abstract shows no statistical claim and a deterministic automaton design. The ratings were made by the author alone.

Omitted are all the dimensions where others lead: adversary in threat model, evaluation on real attacks, utility data, guards actually run, temporal or session properties, enforcement in a real framework. On these Tint scores no or nearly no: "no LLM", no attacks, no temporal properties.

The layered position paper is said to be advanced by "a step toward". Its abstract proposes layers whose probabilistic guarantee discharges the next layer's assumption, with system bounds by the chain rule, and lists non-i.i.d. estimation as open. This paper deliberately avoids composing probabilistic guarantees, so it takes a different route. It should present that as a disagreement.

*Fix.* Add those columns, use four values (Y / partial / N / n/a), have a second rater, and report disagreements. Replace "a step toward" with an honest comparison.

**13. No worked example; the running example is dropped after Section 4.**
*Location:* whole paper.

*What is wrong.* $T^\ast$, $T_b$ and $T_j$ occur only in the introduction, and $T_j^p$ only twice more. $T^\ast$ is never shown being stopped by $g_2$. No HSL listing, no checker output, no countermodel and no certificate appear anywhere, although the paper's value is that a tool produces these. Macros `\resExampleMisses`, `\resExampleBound` and related remain defined but unused, so an example appears to have been removed.

Other inconsistencies:
- `devagent_exposed_judge.json` differs from `devagent.json` in more than the view: it drops $g_3$, $\varphi_{\mathrm{env}}$ and `config_match`. So the compared variants are not a one-factor change, though the text implies they are.
- The prose contract ("no attacker-addressed instructions") differs from the specification's (`iid_calibration`, `config_match`, `structured_view`). The link between the two, that structured view implies no addressed instructions, is never argued.
- $\Omega_2$ is unspecified, and there is no $L_1$.
- Table `tab:cases` is captioned "Published Designs" but includes DevAgent and "Policy inventory", which is not explained anywhere in the paper.
- "Three observations follow" introduces more than three designs and no clear three points.

*Fix.* Add a section tracing all four traces through DevAgent: HSL text, check output for both variants, the generated countermodel as a table, routes, a certificate with numbers, one change and its stale set. Use it again in every later section.

**14. Missing TSE material: how to write contracts, and how to use this.**
*Location:* absent.

*What is wrong.* The target length is 15 to 25 pages and the paper has 12. Its largest practical gap is the step that determines every result: writing goals and obligations. Checker matching is on declared name strings, so a developer gets acceptance by adding a string to `evidence`, as Finding 1 shows. The residual list is useful only if someone must justify each entry.

*Fix.* Add the following, which would bring the paper into range without padding:

1. *Writing contracts* (2 pages): a method for choosing goals independent of tools; rules for obligation granularity; a catalogue by guard class (regex, static analyser, classifier, LLM judge, sandbox, human) giving realistic obligations, what can discharge each, and who owns it; common mistakes, including clean-input obligations on detectors.
2. *Worked example* (2 pages, Finding 13).
3. *Deployment guidance* (1.5 pages): CI integration, what to do on a rejection, a review process for `assumed` entries, rollout and shadow calibration, monitoring for drift, how a failed certificate is handled. Consider requiring an attached argument for each assumed obligation, in the spirit of assurance cases.
4. *Corpus or case study* (2 to 3 pages, Finding 4).
5. *Discussion* (1 page): attacker table, what the method does not buy, when not to use it, comparison with simple review.
6. *Coverage of constructs* (0.5 page, Finding 6).

Move "Where the Assumptions Remain" out of the risk section into a visible section and format it as a table: assumption, needed by, how to discharge, failure consequence. A dense paragraph of italics is the wrong form for the most important practical content.

**15. Effort and emphasis are mismatched.**
*Location:* abstract, title, Secs. `sec:engineering`, `sec:eval`.

*What is wrong.* Twelve of thirteen experiments validate a small algorithm on inputs it was built for. The one that touches the world gets a paragraph, and its experiment is the weakest. Several theorems are close to definitional: Decision cost, Taint, Routes, and the second half of Session invariants. The slowest proof obligation takes 0.01 s, which suggests easy goals. Listing them as eleven rows of proved results inflates the appearance of depth.

Completeness is exact largely because the semantics leaves every stage free, so it is close to true by construction. That is a merit in design, but the abstract presents it as the headline. The abstract's million-node claim contradicts the threats section, which says scale is headroom.

*Fix.* Demote minor theorems to lemmas or a table. Reduce E3 to E8 to a compact section. Drop million-node claims from the abstract in favour of what matters: exact findings, witnesses, residuals, certificates tied to code.

**16. Protocol and provenance statements are stronger than the evidence.**
*Location:* Sec. Setup; Sec. Continuity; Data Availability.

*What is wrong.*
- "The protocol was written before the runs", but `PROTOCOL.md` carries that day's date and `experiments/v2` is untracked in git, so nothing demonstrates the ordering.
- Three case files were edited after first results, which changed verdicts. This is recorded in the errata, but the protocol says post-result analyses are labelled exploratory, and E13 in the paper is not so labelled.
- Table `tab:exps` lists E11's rule as "zero locality violations", which is absent from the protocol's rule for E11.
- The "earlier preregistered sandbox study" has no citation. Claims about it (refusal in all seeds, the provenance rule beating the pipeline, shift failure) cannot be checked by the reader and have no numbers. Yet it is the only evidence in the paper bearing on semantic guards, and it is negative.
- "Link withheld for anonymous review" appears with the author named on the title page.

*Fix.* Commit and timestamp the protocol. Mark E13's revised verdicts as post hoc. Cite the earlier study or give a table. Decide on review mode.

**18. Prose: competent, with a recognisable pattern of AI-like tics.** The prose is plainer than most, and honest. But the same devices recur:

- Aphoristic paragraph endings: "A verdict moves along the hierarchy; the evidence behind it does not." "The price buys incrementality." "This small fact drives Section..." "That is its value." "Trust is not transitive."
- Negation pivots: "The defect is in the use of the verdict, not in any guard"; "a factor, not an asymptotic class"; "It does not mean that..." The word "exact" appears 17 times and "does not" 21 times in about 9,000 tokens.
- Tidy triads and announcements: "The three stage types separate three things", "Three measures address this", "Three observations follow", "The precise reading matters", "is the crux".
- Staged confession as rhetoric: "A proof that passes too easily deserves suspicion, and ours did", "The first run was sobering", "Our first version lacked the disjunct". The honesty is good, the drama is not; keep them in the failures section, in plain words.
- Slogan framing: "A guardrail hierarchy is a program, and it can be verified like one"; "structure is the kind of thing software engineering knows how to specify and verify"; "It moves the argument from 'the stack looks strong' to...".
